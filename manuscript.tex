\begin{abstract}
    This study presents a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) for unknown systems subject to multiple convex input constraints. 
    % A deep neural network (DNN) is employed to approximate the ideal stabilizing control law while simultaneously learning the unknown system dynamics and addressing multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework.
    A deep neural network (DNN) is employed to approximate the ideal stabilizing control law while handling multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework.
    % The adaptation law of DNN weights is formulated to solve the defined constrained optimization problem, ensuring satisfaction of first-order optimality conditions at steady state.
    The DNN weights and associated Lagrange multipliers are updated through adaptation laws derived from the formulated constrained optimization problem, yielding KKT-type first-order optimality conditions at steady state.
    The controller's stability is rigorously analyzed using Lyapunov theory, guaranteeing uniformly ultimately bounded tracking errors, and DNN weights and Lagrange multipliers.
    The proposed controller is compared with existing methods in numerical simulations, highlighting its capability to handle multiple convex input constraints.
    % demonstrating its superiority in terms of tracking performance and convex input constraint satisfaction.
    The feasibility and effectiveness of CONAC in real-world applications are validated through a real-time implementation on a 2-DOF robotic manipulator.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
    Neuro-adaptive control, constrained optimization, deep neural network, convex input constraints.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{rom} a practical perspective, many engineering systems are constrained by multiple safety requirements, actuator limitations, and physical restrictions \cite{Ji:2025aa}.
These constraints often manifest as convex input constraints, which can be in the form of nonlinear constraints rather than simple box constraints that limit each input independently.
For example, under road friction limits, the admissible global force and moment of a vehicle can be characterized by a weighted norm bound, which yields a convex ellipsoidal constraint \cite{Chen:2024aa}.

% \cred{
% For instance, due to power limitations for actuators, robotic manipulators may have constraints on the upper bounds on the norm of the joint torques.
% }
Moreover, in motor drive systems, the feasible voltage region in motor drives is often characterized by circular \cite{Ryu:2025aa} or hexagonal sets \cite{Monzen:2026aa}.
% More recently, input constraints induced by state constraints using control barrier functions (CBFs) \cite{Ames:2019aa} are inherently nonlinear and complex.
Moreover, input limits may be determined by multiple constraints that must hold simultaneously, such as component-wise bounds combined with norm constraints, which can be represented as the intersection of multiple convex sets.
Therefore, addressing these convex input constraints is crucial for ensuring the safety and reliability of control systems in real-world applications.

In parallel, another challenge in engineering systems is uncertain dynamics or even completely unknown dynamics, which can arise from modeling errors, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.
Although adaptive control provides a systematic mechanism for online adaptation of controllers \cite{Ioannou:2006aa}, conventional formulations often rely on structural assumptions on the uncertain or unknown dynamics, which may not be available in practice.
Neuro-adaptive control (NAC) alleviates this limitation by using neural networks (NNs) to approximate the dynamics in a structured and parameterized representation \cite{Farrell:2006aa}.
Like adaptive control, NAC methods ensure closed-loop stability using Lyapunov theory, and the NN weights are adapted online without prior knowledge of the system dynamics.
The NAC methods have shown promising results in various real-world applications, including flexible robotic manipulators \cite{Gao:2026aa}, active suspensions \cite{Zhao:2016aa}, synchronous machine drives \cite{Ryu:2025aa}, and swarm control of mobile robots \cite{Yu:2022aa}.

Because multiple convex input constraints and uncertain or unknown dynamics often coexist in real-world applications, incorporating input constraint handling into NAC is essential.
This motivates a review of existing input constraint handling methods in NAC and related constrained control frameworks.

% STRUCTURAL METHODS

\subsubsection{Structural Methods for Input Constraint Handling in NAC}

In NAC, structural methods have been developed for input constraint handling, mainly because they preserve the continuous-time controller structure and are amenable to Lyapunov stability analysis.
Auxiliary-system-based approaches are the most widely used methods owing to their simplicity and effectiveness \cite{Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Chen:2011aa,He:2016aa,Zhao:2016aa,Gao:2026aa,Yu:2022aa,Liu:2025aa,Ma:2025aa}.
When input saturation occurs, the auxiliary state is excited and evolves according to the auxiliary system, whose dynamics may be linear \cite{Yu:2022aa}, nonlinear \cite{Chen:2011aa,Gao:2026aa,Ma:2025aa} or fixed-time-inspired \cite{Liu:2025aa}.
Typically, the excited auxiliary state is directly fed back into the control law as additional compensation terms.

However, from a learning perspective, introducing additional compensation terms into the control law can interfere with the NN adaptation process.
This occurs because the adaptation law typically depends on the feedback error, which may become less informative for updating the NN weights when the additional terms dominate the closed-loop response.
To address this issue, some studies incorporated the auxiliary state into the feedback error used in adaptation laws \cite{Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa}.
This approach allows the NN weights to adapt not only for approximating the ideal control law but also for compensating the effect of input saturation.

Additionally, several other structural methods have been introduced for handling input constraints in NAC. 
For instance, in \cite{Gao:2006aa}, the saturated amount of control input is estimated using NNs and compensated in the control law.
In \cite{Li:2024aa}, the control law is augmented with a smooth saturation function, such as $\tanh(\cdot)$, to accommodate input saturation effects, while the closed-loop stability is analyzed using Lyapunov theory.
For strict-feedback systems, command filters have also been used to limit the magnitude and rate of virtual control inputs in backstepping-based NAC designs \cite{Chen:2011aa}, thereby indirectly handling input constraints.

Barrier Lyapunov function (BLF)-based approaches have also been studied for constrained control problems \cite{Zhao:2025aa,Yang:2021aa}.
BLF-based methods are particularly effective for enforcing state-, output-, or error-related constraints.
When input saturation is involved, they are often combined with auxiliary systems or additional compensation mechanisms.
For instance, in \cite{Yang:2021aa}, the auxiliary system is additionally incorporated into the BLF-based NAC design to handle input saturation.

Despite their effectiveness, most structural NAC methods that explicitly address input saturation are tied to simple box input constraints, such as symmetric or asymmetric bounds on each input.
Thus, extending them to admissible input sets described by multiple nonlinear convex constraints is not straightforward.

% ================================
% OPTIMIZATION-BASED METHODS
% ===============================

\subsubsection{Optimization-Based Input Constraint Handling}

Besides structural methods, optimization-based methods have been widely used for handling input constraints across a broad range of control design frameworks.
These methods compute the control input by solving a constrained optimization problem at each time step, as in model predictive control (MPC) \cite{Draeger:1995aa,Chen:2024aa,Cetin:2019aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa} and control barrier function (CBF)-based quadratic programming (QP) filters \cite{Ames:2019aa,Ko:2025aa,Sweatland:2025aa}. 
Because the control decision is explicitly formulated as a constrained optimization problem, complex nonlinear input constraints can be systematically incorporated into the control design, which is a significant advantage over structural methods.

Despite this advantage, the closed-loop guarantees typically rely on the feasibility of a model-based constrained optimization problem.
When the system dynamics are uncertain or unknown, feasibility certified by the nominal model may not directly carry over to the real system.
To reduce this model dependence, NNs have been used to learn unknown dynamics from measured data offline \cite{Draeger:1995aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa} or to approximate constrained optimal control policies \cite{Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa}.
However, these guarantees depend on the NN approximation error and the operating domain covered by the training data; outside this domain, the learned model or policy may lead to degraded performance or weakened guarantees.

To compensate for residual uncertainties and policy-approximation errors, some studies further introduce online correction terms \cite{Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa} or online NN adaptation \cite{Cetin:2019aa}.
Although the closed-loop stability of such online schemes can be analyzed using Lyapunov arguments, guaranteeing that the final corrected input satisfies the prescribed input constraints is nontrivial because the correction or adaptation terms are not necessarily designed to remain inside the admissible input set.
CBF-QP filters can be employed to enforce safety or input-related constraints \cite{Sweatland:2025aa}.
However, because the filtered input may differ from the NN-generated input, the filtering process can complicate the interaction between NN adaptation and constraint handling.

\subsection{Motivation and Proposed Approach}

Both structural and optimization-based methods are effective for input-constraint handling, but they have complementary limitations.
Structural methods offer computational efficiency and Lyapunov-based stability guarantees, but they are limited in the class of constraints they can accommodate.
Optimization-based methods can handle complex constraints, but their closed-loop stability guarantees are often tied to model-dependent feasibility conditions.
Moreover, when online adaptation is combined with constrained filters, the filtered input may differ from the NN output, which can interfere with adaptation.

These observations motivate a unified NAC framework in which convex input constraints are incorporated directly into the online adaptation law, rather than being handled through additional structural components or separate optimization-based filters as presented in \cite{Ryu:2025ab}.
In the proposed CONAC framework, the NAC problem is formulated as a constrained optimization problem in which multiple convex input constraints are imposed as inequality constraints.
The constrained optimization formulation is not used to solve an online control allocation problem at each sampling instant; instead, it is used to derive continuous-time adaptation laws for the DNN weights and the associated Lagrange multipliers.
Therefore, the proposed framework retains the Lyapunov-friendly structure of NAC while incorporating the constraint-handling capability of optimization-based methods.

% Constrained optimization provides a theoretical framework for defining optimality and presents numerical methods for finding solutions that satisfy the constraints \cite{Nocedal:2006aa}.

\subsection{Contributions}

The main contributions of this study are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 
    A constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) framework is proposed for unknown systems under multiple convex input constraints. 
    The admissible input set is represented by convex inequality constraints and embedded directly into the online DNN adaptation law;

    \item 
    The proposed framework accommodates combinations of convex input constraints satisfying the stated assumptions. 
    The adaptation laws for the DNN weights and the associated Lagrange multipliers are systematically derived from the formulated constrained optimization problem. 
    At an equilibrium of the adaptation dynamics, the resulting conditions correspond to KKT-type first-order optimality conditions for the formulated constrained adaptation problem, in the sense of \cite[Chap. 12, Thm. 12.1]{Nocedal:2006aa}; and

    \item 
    Lyapunov-based stability analysis is provided to establish boundedness of the tracking error, DNN weights, and Lagrange multipliers. 
    The effectiveness and real-time feasibility of the proposed CONAC are validated through simulations and experiments on a 2-DOF robotic manipulator.
\end{itemize}

\hfill

This study extends our preliminary work in \cite{Ryu:2025ab,Ryu:2025aa}, which introduced inequality constraints on the weight and input norms within a constrained optimization framework for neuro-adaptive control (NAC) systems using single hidden layer NNs. 
In the present work, the same framework is generalized to accommodate arbitrary convex input constraints and extended to DNNs, while still retaining the capability to regulate the weight norm. 
Furthermore, unlike the previous study, which was limited to numerical simulations, the proposed CONAC is experimentally validated through real-time implementation on a 2-DOF robotic manipulator.

\section{Problem Formulation} \label{sec:problem:formulation}

We consider an unknown nonlinear system modeled as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sys}
    \ddtt\mv{x} = \mv{f}(\mv{x}) + \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u})
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{x},\mv{u}\in\R^n$ denote state and control input vectors, respectively, and $\mv{f}:\R^n\to\R^n$ and $\mm{B}:\R^n\to\R^{n\times n}$ denote unknown system function and control effectiveness matrix, which are locally Lipschitz continuous, respectively.
% The compact set $\Omega_x$ denotes a approximation region where NN will approximate with minimum bounded approximation error, later.
The saturation function $\mysat:\R^n\to\Omega_u$ radially projects the input onto the admissible input set $\Omega_u$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:saturation}.

The system \eqref{eq:sys} is subject to the following assumptions.

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound}
    The control effectiveness matrix $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ is nonsingular and bounded as   
    $
        \underline{b}\mm{I}_n
        \preceq 
        \mm{B}(\mv{x})
        \preceq 
        \bar{b}\mm{I}_n
        ,
    $
    where $\underline{b},\bar{b}\in\R_{>0}$ are known positive constants.\
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound:grad}
    \cite[Assumption 1(b)]{Boulkroune:2010aa}
    There exists an unknown positive function $\beta(\mv{x}):\R^n\to\R_{>0}$ such that $\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{v}^\top\left(\ddtt\mm{B}(\mv{x})^{-1}\right)\mv{v} \le \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{v}}^2$, $\forall \mv{v}\in\R^n$ and $\forall \mv{x}\in\R^n$.
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:sat}
    The admissible input set $\Omega_u$ is convex and defined by multiple convex functions $c_j^{u}(\mv{u}),\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I},$ where each $c_j^{u}(\mv{u})$ is $\mathcal{C}^1$ and $\mathcal{I}$ is an input constraint index set.
    Thus, the admissible input set is defined as
    $
        \Omega_u 
        = 
        \bigcap_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \left\{ \mv{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n : c_j^{u}(\mv{u}) \leq 0 \right\}
        .
    $ 
    In addition, the admissible input set contains the origin in its interior, \ie $c_j^{u}(\mv{0})<0,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$.
    Moreover, the norm of any boundary point of $\Omega_u$ is bounded, such that $0<\underline{u}\le\norm{\mv{u}}\le\overline{u},\ \forall\mv{u}\in\partial\Omega_u$, where $\partial \Omega_u$ denotes the boundary of $\Omega_u$.
\end{assum}

We review the DNN structure and its approximation capability, which are essential for the subsequent controller design and stability analysis.
For smooth unknown nonlinear functions $\mv{g}:\R^{l_0+1}\to\R^{l_{k+1}}$, DNNs $\NN: \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1} \times \mathbb{R}^{\Xi} \to \mathbb{R}^{l_k+1}$ can be used to approximate $\mv{g}$ \cite{Patil:2022aa}.
According to the universal approximation theorem, there exists a DNN such that 
\begin{equation}
    \sup_{\mv{x}_{NN}\in\Omega_{\mv{x}_{NN}}}
    \norm{
        \mv{g}(\mv{x}_{NN}) - \NN(\mv{x}_{NN};\wth^*)
    }
    =
    \overline{\epsilon}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{x}_{NN} \in \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ is the DNN input vector, $\Omega_{\mv{x}_{NN}} \subset \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ is a compact set, and $\idealwth \in \mathbb{R}^{\Xi}$ denotes the ideal DNN weights that minimize the approximation error $\overline{\epsilon} > 0$.
The DNN can be defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dnn:def}
    \NN_i :=
    \begin{cases}
        \wV_i^\top \act_i(\NN_{i-1}), 
        &
        i\in\{1,\dots ,k\}=:\mathcal{K},
        \\
        \wV_0^\top {x}_{NN},
        &
        i=0
        ,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\wV_i\in\R^{(l_i+1)\times l_{i+1}}$ and $\act_i: \R^{l_i}\to\R^{l_i+1}$ represent the weight matrix and the activation function of the $i$th layer for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$, respectively, and $\mathcal{K}$ denotes the index set of layers.
The DNN $\NN({x}_{NN};\wth)$ has $k+1$ layers, where the input and output layers are indexed by $i=0$ and $i=k$, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:DNN} as an example.
The activation function is defined as $\act_i(\mv{z}) = \left(\tanh(z_1),\cdots,\tanh(z_{l_i}),1\right)^\top,\ \forall\mv{z}\in\R^{l_i}$.
We simplify the notations by vectorizing all weight matrices as $\wth_i := \myvec(\wV_i)\in\R^{(l_i+1)l_{i+1}},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}$, and, finally, stacking them as $\wth := \left[\wth_i\right]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$, where $\Xi := \sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}} (l_i+1)l_{i+1}$.

The gradient of $ \NN({\mv{x}}_{NN};\wth)$ with respect to $\wth$ can be obtained using Proposition \ref{propsit:kron} and the chain rule as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth}=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_k}&
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_{k-1}}&
    \cdots &
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_0}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in\R^{l_{k+1} \times \Xi}
    ,
    \label{eq:gradient:phi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth_i} = 
    \begin{cases}
        % (
            \mm I_{l_{k+1}}
            \otimes 
            \act_{k}^\top  
        % )
        , 
        &
        i=k
        ,
        \\
        \wV_k^\top\act_{k}' 
        (
            \mm I_{l_{k}}
            \otimes  
            \act_{k-1}^\top  
        )
        , 
        & 
        i=k-1
        ,
        \\
        \vdots 
        &
        \vdots 
        \\
        \wV_k^\top\act'_{k} 
        \cdots 
        \wV_c^\top\act_1' 
        (
            \mm I_{l_1}
            \otimes 
            {\mv{x}}_{NN}^\top  
        )
        , 
        &
        i = 0
        ,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:NN:grad}
\end{equation}
and $\act_i:= \act_i(\NN_{i-1})$ and $\act_i':= \ddtfrac{\act_i}{\NN_{i-1}}$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$.

\subsection{Controller Design} \label{sec:main:result:sub:ctrl:design}

We define the tracking error as $\mv{e} = \mv{x} - \mv{x}_d$, and derive the error dynamics from \eqref{eq:sys} as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:error:dyn}
    \ddtt\mv{e}
    =
    \mv{f}(\mv{x}) + \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u}) - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    .
\end{equation}
Let us consider Lyapunov function $V_c:=\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}(\mv{x})\mv{e}$.
Taking time derivative of $V_c$ yields
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vc:dot:1} \nonumber
    \ddtt V_c
    =&
    \tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top
    \left(
        \ddtt\mm{B}^{-1}
    \right)
    \mv{e}
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        \mv{f} + \mm{B}\mysat(\mv{u}) 
        - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    \\
    \le&
    \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{e}}^2
    \!+\!
    \mv{e}^\top\mv{u}
    \!+\!
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        \mv{f} \!-\! \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    \!+\!
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta\mv{u}
    ,
\end{align}
where arguments of $\mv{f}(\mv{x})$ and $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ are omitted for brevity and $\Delta\mv{u} := \mysat(\mv{u}) - \mv{u}$ denotes amount of input saturation.

A desired control law $\mv{u}_d$ such that the tracking error $\mv{e}$ is stable in the absence of uncertainties and input saturation, is selected as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ctrl:ideal}
    \mv{u}_d
    :=
    -
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
    - 
    \beta(\mv{x})\mv{e}
    +
    \mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        - \mv{f} 
        + \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    % +
    % h(\mv{x},\mv{x}_d)
    ,
\end{equation}
where user-defined matrix $\mm{P}=\mm{P}^\top\in\R^{n\times n}$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix satisfying $\underline{p}\mm{I}_n \preceq \mm{P} \preceq \overline{p}\mm{I}_n$ for known positive constants $\underline{p},\overline{p}\in\R_{>0}$.
% The function $h:\R^n\times\R^n\to\R^n$ is correction term to satisfy the ideal control law $\mv{u}^*$ within the input constraints, \ie $\mv{u}^*\in\Omega_u$.

By substituting $\mv{u}_d$ \eqref{eq:ctrl:ideal} into \eqref{eq:Vc:dot:1}, we obtain
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vc:dot:2} 
    \ddtt V_c
    \le &
    -\mv{e}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \left(
        \mv{u} - \mv{u}_d
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta\mv{u}
    \\ \nonumber
    \le & 
    -
    \underline{p}\norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \left(
        \mv{u} - \mv{u}_d       
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    % \left(
        \Delta\mv{u}
    %     +
    %     h(\mv{x},\mv{x}_d)
    % \right)
    .
\end{align}
Notably, in ideal case, \ie $\mv{u} = \mv{u}_d$ and $\Delta\mv{u} = \mv{0}$, the tracking error $\mv{e}$ is asymptotically stable, since $\ddtt V_c \le -\underline{p}\norm{\mv{e}}^2$.

\subsection{Adaptation Laws} \label{sec:main:result:sub:adap:laws}

% The control objective which minimizes the tracking error $\mv{e}$ while satisfying both weight boundedness and input constraints can be represented as follows:
The control objectives defined in Section \ref{sec:problem:formulation} is represented as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:opt:prob} 
    \begin{align} 
        \min_{\estwth} \quad 
        & 
        J(\mv{e};\estwth):= \tfrac{1}{2} \mv{e}^\top \mv{e} \label{eq:opt:prob:obj} 
        \\ 
        \text{subject to} \quad 
        & 
        c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i):=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\estwth_i}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\theta}_i^2 \le 0, 
        \  \forall i\in\mathcal{K} \label{eq:opt:prob:const1} 
        \\ 
        & 
        c^{u}_j(\estwth)
        % c_j^u
        % \left(
        %     \mm{P}\NN(\mv{x}_{NN};\estwth)
        % \right) 
        \le 0, 
        \quad \forall j\in\mathcal{I} \label{eq:opt:prob:const2} 
        ,
    \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $J(\mv{e};\estwth)$ is the objective function.
Inequality constraints of weight boundedness \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const1} and input saturation \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2} are imposed during the weight adaptation process.

Specifically, the input constraints used in \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2} are the same convex constraint functions that characterize the admissible input set in Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}.
With a slight abuse of notation, the composed input constraint is denoted by
$c_j^u(\estwth):=c_j^u(\mv u(\estwth))=c_j^u(\mm P\NN(\mv x_{NN};\estwth)),\ \forall j\in\mathcal I$, to explicitly represent the dependence of the input constraints on the DNN weights $\estwth$.
Additionally, all active constraints are assumed to satisfy the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) to avoid degeneracy \cite[pp. 465--467]{Nocedal:2006aa}.

The Lagrangian function $L:=L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    L
    = 
    J(\mv{e};\estwth) 
    + 
    \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
    \sigma_{i}
    c^{\theta}_{i}(\estwth_i)
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}
    \lambda_{j}
    c^{u}_{j}(\estwth)
    ,
    \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ denote the Lagrange multipliers for each weight \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const1} and input constraints \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2}, respectively.
Then, the primal-dual saddle-point problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:opt:prob:saddle}
    \min_{\estwth}
    \max_{
        [\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
        ,
        [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    } 
    L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})
    .
\end{equation}

The adaptation laws for $\estwth$, and $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ are derived to solve \eqref{eq:opt:prob:saddle} by using a primal-dual gradient update law, \ie 
$
    \ddtt\estwth 
    = 
    -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}
$, $
    \ddtt\sigma_i 
    = 
    \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
    \left(
        \beta_i^\theta 
        \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i}
    \right)
$, and $
    \ddtt\lambda_j 
    = 
    \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
    \left(
        \beta_j^u 
        \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j}
    \right)
$, resulting in
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:adap}
    \begin{align}
            \ddtt{\estwth}
            &
            % =
            % -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}
            =
            -\alpha 
            \left(
                \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
                +
                \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
                \sigma_i 
                \estwth_i
                +
                \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
                \lambda_j 
                \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\estwth}
            \right),
        \label{eq:adap:th}
            \\
            \ddtt\sigma_i
            & 
            % = 
            % \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
            % \left(
            %     \beta_i \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i} 
            % \right)
            = 
            \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
                \left(
                \beta_i^{\theta}
                c^{\theta}_{i} 
            \right)
            ,
            \quad \forall i\in\mathcal{K},
        \label{eq:adap:sig}
            \\
            \ddtt\lambda_j
            & 
            % = 
            % \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
            % \left(
            %     \beta_j \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j} 
            % \right)
            = 
            \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
                \left(
                \beta_j^{u}
                c^{u}_{j} 
            \right)
            ,
            \quad \forall j\in\mathcal{I},
        \label{eq:adap:lbd}
    \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $\alpha\in\R_{>0}$ denotes the adaptation gain (learning rate) and $\beta_i^{\theta},\beta_j^{u}\in\R_{>0}$ denote the update rate of the Lagrange multipliers. 
The Lagrange multipliers associated with inequality constraints are kept non-negative, \ie $\sigma_i,\lambda_j\ge 0$, due to the projection operator $\myproj_{(\cdot)\ge0}(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd} \cite[Appendix E, eq. (E.4)]{Krstic:1995aa}.
% To ensure Lipschitz continuity, smooth projection operators are employed, which are presented in \cite[Appendix E, eq. (E.4)]{Krstic:1995aa}.

When a constraint becomes violated, \ie positive constraint violation $c^{\theta}_{i},c^{u}_{j}\ge0$, the corresponding Lagrange multiplier increases, see \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}, to address the violation by updating weight $\estwth$, as shown in \eqref{eq:adap:th}.
Ultimately, the Lagrange multiplier exhibits two distinct behaviors depending on the constraint status: it either decreases to zero (\eg via a projection operator) when the constraint becomes inactive, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j < 0$, or converges to a positive value when the constraint remains binding, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j = 0$, to enforce the limit.

The above primal-dual interpretation also provides an optimality interpretation of the proposed adaptation laws at equilibrium.
At an equilibrium of the adaptation law \eqref{eq:adap}, where $\ddtt{\estwth}=0$, $\ddtt\sigma_i=0$ and $\ddtt\lambda_j=0$, the resulting equilibrium conditions correspond to KKT-type conditions, \ie $\pptfrac{L}{\estwth}=0$, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j \le 0$, $\sigma_i,\lambda_j \ge 0$, and $\sigma_i c^{\theta}_i=0$, and $\lambda_j c^{u}_j=0$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$ and $j\in\mathcal{I}$.
In other words, the proposed adaptation laws update $\estwth$, and $\sigma_i$ and $\lambda_j$ in a way that satisfies the KKT-type conditions at the equilibrium.
These conditions can be interpreted as KKT-type first-order optimality conditions and characterize candidates for locally optimal equilibria.
% These conditions represent first-order necessary conditions for the formulated constrained adaptation problem and characterize candidates for locally optimal equilibria.

\hfill

The adaptation law of $\estwth$ defined in \eqref{eq:adap:th} involves the partial derivative of the error with respect to control input $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$, \ie 
$
    \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
    =
    \left(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        (\pptfrac{\mv{u}}{\estwth})
    \right)
    ^\top \mv{e}
    =
    \left(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        \mm{P}
        (\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth})
    \right)
    ^\top \mv{e}
$. 
Since the objective function depends on $\mv{e}$ of a dynamic system, obtaining $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ is not straightforward. 
Exact sensitivity computation methods, such as the forward sensitivity method \cite{Khalil:2002aa,Ryu:2025ab}, can be used in principle, but they are often computationally demanding for real-time applications with numerous DNN weights.
Therefore, several approximation methods have been proposed in the literature \cite{Douratsos:2007aa,Saerens:1991aa}.
In the literature, the authors approximate $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ by taking the sign of each entry, \ie
$
    \pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}
    \approx
    \left[
        \mysign
        \left(
            \pptfrac{e_i}{u_j}
        \right)
    \right]_{i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}}
    ,
$
which corresponds to MIT rule in the context of adaptive control.
However, since this approach is not applicable to \eqref{eq:sys}, we instead use the approximation $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}\approx \mm{I}_n$ under Assumption \ref{assum:B:bound}, given that the sign of the control effectiveness matrix is known.
Consequently, the adaptation law in \eqref{eq:adap:th} becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \ddtt{\estwth}
    \approx
    -\alpha 
    \left(
        {\pptfrac{\estNN}
        {\estwth}}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \mv{e}
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
        \sigma_i 
        \estwth_i
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j 
        \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\estwth}
    \right)
    .
    \label{eq:adap:th:approx}
\end{equation}

\section{Stability Analysis} \label{sec:stability:analysis}

In this section, the stability of the closed-loop system is analyzed.
To facilitate the stability analysis, we introduce the following lemmas.

\begin{lem}[Convexity of Input Constraints with respect to $\wth_k$] \label{lem:convexity:input:constraint}
    The input constraint function $c_j^{u}(\wth_k),\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$ is convex with respect to the output layer weights $\wth_k$, resulting in inequality $c_j^{u}(\wth_k) - \pptfrac{c_j^{u}}{\wth_k}^\top \wth_k \le c_j^{u}(\wth)\big|_{\wth_k=\mv{0}}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
    % The dependence of the preceding layers and $\mv{x}_{NN}$ on the output layer appears through the activation vector $\act_k$.
    Since $\act_k(\cdot)$ employs the bounded activation function $\tanh(\cdot)$, we have 
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{\act_k(\cdot)} \le \sqrt{l_k+1}
        .
    \end{equation}
    Therefore, using the DNN gradient in \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}, we have
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:NN:linear}
        \NN
        =
        \frac{
            \partial \NN
        }{
            \partial\wth_k
        }
        \wth_k
        = 
        \left(
            \mm{I}_{l_{k+1}} \otimes \act_k^\top
        \right)
        \wth_k
        .
    \end{equation}
    This implies that $\mv{u}=\mm{P}\NN$ depends linearly on $\wth_k$.
    Therefore, the convexity of $c_j^u(\wth),\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$, with respect to $\mv{u}$ is preserved with respect to $\wth_k$ due to the linear dependence of $\mv{u}$ on $\wth_k$.
    In addition, the convexity of $c_j^u(\wth)$ with respect to $\wth_k$ implies that $c_j^u(\wth) - \pptfrac{c_j^u}{\wth}^\top \wth_k \le c_j^u(\wth)\big|_{\wth_k=\mv{0}}$, which concludes the proof.
    % , where $c_j^u(\mv{0})$ denotes the value at $\wth_k=\mv{0}$
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Boundedness of $\Delta\mv{u}$ with respect to $\estwth_k$] \label{lem:delta:u:bound}
    The saturated amount of control input $\Delta\mv{u}$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as $\norm{\Delta\mv{u}}\le \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
    According to \eqref{eq:NN:linear}, the control input $\mv{u}$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as follows:
    \begin{equation} \label{eq:NN:bound}
        \norm{\mv{u}} 
        \le
        \overline{p}
        \norm{\estNN}
        \le
        \overline{p}
        \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
        .   
    \end{equation}

    Since $\mysat(\cdot)$ projects the input onto $\Omega_u$ along the radial direction, if $\mv u\notin\Omega_u$, then $\mysat(\mv u)\in\partial\Omega_u$ lies on the same ray from the origin as $\mv u$.
    Therefore,
    $
        \norm{\Delta\mv u}
        =
        \norm{\mv u}-\norm{\mysat(\mv u)}
    $.
    Moreover, by Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}, $\norm{\mysat(\mv u)}\ge\underline u$ holds for $\mysat(\mv u)\in\partial\Omega_u$.
    Hence,
    $
        \norm{\Delta\mv u}
        \le
        \norm{\mv u}-\underline u
    $
    when $\mv u\notin\Omega_u$, while $\Delta\mv u=\mv 0$ when $\mv u\in\Omega_u$.
    Thus, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{\Delta\mv{u}}
        \le
        \max
        \left\{
            \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
            -
            \underline{u}
            ,
            0
        \right\}
        \le
        \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
        ,
    \end{equation}
    which concludes the proof.

\end{proof}

The following theorem establishes the uniformly ultimately boundedness (UUB) of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{I}$ and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:stability}
    Consider the closed-loop system consisting of \eqref{eq:sys}, the control law \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:actual}, and the adaptation laws \eqref{eq:adap}.
    Suppose that Assumptions~\ref{assum:B:bound}--\ref{assum:sat} hold.
    Then, the tracking error $\mv e$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{K}$ and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$ are uniformly ultimately bounded (UUB).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

The proof is divided into two steps.
We first analyze the UBB of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the output-layer weights $\estwth_k$, and the corresponding Lagrange multiplier $\sigma_k$, and the Lagrange multipliers associated with the input constraints $\lambda_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$.
This step provides the key boundedness result for the tracking error and the controller output.
In the following step, we then extend the analysis to the remaining inner-layer weights, and Lagrange multipliers associated with the weight constraints, recursively.

\hfill

\textit{Step 1: Boundedness of the Tracking Error, Output-Layer Variables, and Input-Constraint Multipliers.}

% For notational simplicity in this subsection, $c_j^u(\estwth_k)$ denotes the dependence of the input constraint $c_j^u(\mm P\estNN)$ on the output-layer weight $\estwth_k$.
% The remaining dependence on $\mv{x}_{NN}$ and the preceding-layer weights enters through the activation vector $\act_k$, whose entries are bounded due to the use of $\tanh(\cdot)$ activation functions.

Consider the following Lyapunov function 
$
    V_{k}(\mv{z}_k)
    :=
    V_c
    +
    \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\estwth_k^\top\estwth_k
    +
    \tfrac{1}{\beta_k^\theta}(\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o)^2
    +
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \tfrac{1}{2\beta_j^{u}}\lambda_j^2
    ,
$
where 
$
    \mv{z}_k
    :=
    \left(
        \mv{e}^\top,
        \estwth_k^\top,
        \sigma_k,
        \left[
            \lambda_j
        \right]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}^\top
    \right)^\top
$ and $\sigma_k^o\in\R_{>0}$ will be determined later.
The Lyapunov function $V_k:=V_k(\mv{z}_k)$ is lower bounded and radially unbounded with respect to $\mv{z}_k$.
In the following analysis, we consider the case where the output-layer weight constraint is violated, \ie $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)>0$.
If this condition does not hold, then $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)\le0$ implies $\norm{\estwth_k}\le\overline{\theta}_k$, and the boundedness of $\estwth_k$ follows directly.

Invoking Lemma \ref{lem:convexity:input:constraint}, time derivative of $V_{k}$ can be analyzed as follows:
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:1} \nonumber
    \ddtt V_{k}
    \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{P}
    \left(
        \estNN - \idealNN
        - \mv{\epsilon}
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta \mv{u}
    \\ \nonumber
    &
    -
    \underbrace{
        \estwth_k^\top
        \pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_k}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \mv{e}
    }_{
        = 
        \estNN^\top\mm{P}\mv{e}
        ,\ \text{see, \eqref{eq:NN:linear}}
    }
    -
    \sigma_k
    \estwth_k^\top
    \estwth_k
    -    
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    \estwth_k^\top
    \pptfrac{c^{u}_j}{\estwth_k}
    \\ % \nonumber
    &
    +
    2(\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o) c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j c^{u}_j(\estwth)
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \left(
        - \idealNN
        - \mv{\epsilon}
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta \mv{u}
    -
    \sigma_k \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    \\ \nonumber
    &
    +
    (\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o) 
    \left(
        \norm{\estwth_k}^2
        -
        \overline{\theta}_k^{2}
    \right)
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
    \\ \nonumber
    =&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \left(
        - \idealNN
        - \mv{\epsilon}
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta \mv{u}
    \\ \nonumber
    &
    -
    \sigma_k^o
    \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    -
    \sigma_k
    \overline{\theta}_k^2
    +
    \sigma_k^o
    \overline{\theta}_k^2
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
    .
\end{align}
In \eqref{eq:Vk:dot:1}, the projection operator is omitted, since $\tfrac{1}{\beta_k^{\theta}}(\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o)\cdot\ddtt\sigma_k \le (\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o) c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\beta_j^{u}}\lambda_j\cdot\ddtt\lambda_j = \lambda_j c^{u}_j(\estwth)$ hold, for the projection onto the nonnegative orthant.

For the boundedness of $\idealwth_k$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$, we assume that $\mv{x}_{NN}$ remains in the approximation region $\Omega_{NN}$, which will be ensured later in the proof, and hence $\idealwth_k$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$ are bounded by $\overline{\theta}_k^*$ and $\overline{\epsilon}$, respectively.
Using Lemma \ref{lem:delta:u:bound}, we can further analyze $\ddtt V_{k}$ as follows:
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:2} 
    \ddtt V_{k}
    \le&
        -\underline{p}
        \norm{\mv{e}}^2
        -
        \sigma_k^o
        \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    \\ \nonumber
    &
        +
        \overline{p}
        \norm{\mv{e}}
        \left(
            \overline{\theta}^*_k
            \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
            +
            \overline{\epsilon}
            +
            \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
        \right)        
    \\ \nonumber
    &
        -
        \sigma_k 
        \overline{\theta}_k^2
        +
        \sigma_k^o 
        \overline{\theta}_k^2
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j
        c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
        .
\end{align}

For any $\eta>0$, Young's inequality states that
\begin{equation}
    ab
    \le
    \eta a^2
    +
    \frac{1}{4\eta}b^2,
    \quad
    \forall a,b\in\R_{\ge0}
    .
\end{equation}
Hence, by applying the Young's inequality to third term in the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:Vk:dot:2}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:3} \nonumber
    \ddtt V_{k}
    \le &
        -(\underline{p}-\eta_1-\eta_2)
        \norm{\mv{e}}^2
        -
        \left(
            \sigma_k^o
            -
            \tfrac{1}{4\eta_1}
            \overline{p}^2
            (l_k+1)
        \right)
        \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    \\ 
    &
        -
        \sigma_k \overline{\theta}_k^2
        +
        \textstyle \sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j
        \underbrace{
            c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
        }_{=: \underline{c}_j < 0}
    \\ \nonumber
    &
        +
    \underbrace{
        \tfrac{1}{4\eta_2}
        \left[
        \overline{p}
            \left(
                \overline{\theta}_k^*
                \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
                +
                \overline{\epsilon}
            \right)
        \right]^2
        +
        \sigma_k^o
        \overline{\theta}_k^2
    }_{=: \delta \in \R_{>0}}
    % \\ \nonumber
    % \le &
    %     -\left(
    %         \underline{p}
    %         -
    %         1
    %     \right)
    %     \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    %     -
    %     \tfrac{1}{2}
    %     \sigma_k 
    %     \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    %     -
    %     \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_k \overline{\theta}_k^2
    % \\ \nonumber
    % &
    %     +
    %     \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    %     \lambda_j
    %     c^{u}_j(\mv{0})
    %     +
    %     \delta
    ,
\end{align}
where $\eta_1,\eta_2\in\R_{>0}$ and $\sigma_k^o\in\R_{>0}$ are chosen such that $\underline{p}-\eta_1-\eta_2>0$ for given $\underline{p}$, and $\sigma_k^o - \frac{1}{4\eta_1} \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1) > 0$, respectively.
The constants $\underline{c}_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I},$ are negative, since $\estwth_k=\mv 0$ implies $\estNN=\mv 0$ and hence $\mv u=\mv 0$, resulting in $c_j^u(\estwth)\big|_{\estwth_zk=\mv 0}=c_j^u(\mv 0)<0$ by Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}.

This result implies that $\ddtt V_{k}$ is negative outside the compact set defined by 
\begin{equation}
    \Omega
    \!
    :=
    \!
    \left\{
        \!
        \mv{z}_k
        \!
        :
        \!
        a
        \norm{
            \left(
                \!
                \mv{e}^\top
                \!
                ,
                \!
                \estwth_k^\top
                \!
            \right)^\top
        }^2
        \!
        +
        \!
        b\norm{
            \left(
                \sigma_k
                \!
                ,
                \!
                [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}^\top
            \right)^\top
        }_1
        \!
        \le
        \!
        \delta
        \!
    \right\}
    ,
\end{equation}
where
$
    a
    :=
    \min
    \left\{
        \underline{p}
        - \eta_1
        - \eta_2
        ,
        \sigma_k^o
        -
        \tfrac{1}{4\eta_1}
        \overline{p}^2
        (l_k+1)
    \right\}
    \in\R_{>0}
    ,
    b
    :=
    \min
    \left\{
        \overline{\theta}_k^2
        ,
        \min_{j\in\mathcal{I}} 
        \left\{
            -\underline{c}_j
        \right\}
    \right\}
    \in\R_{>0}
    .
$
% and
% $
%     \mv{z}_1 
%     := 
%     \left(
%         \mv{e}^\top
%         ,
%         \estwth_k^\top
%     \right)^\top
%     ,

Moreover, since $V_k$ is lower bounded and radially unbounded, there exists a compact sublevel set
$
    \mathcal S_k(\overline V_k)
    :=
    \left\{ 
        \mv{z}_k 
        : 
        V_k(\mv{z}_k)
        \le 
        \overline{V}_k

    ,
$
that contains $\Omega$.
Because $\ddtt V_k<0$ for all $\mv{z}_k\notin\Omega$, the trajectory enters and remains in $\mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$ in finite time.
Therefore, $\mv e$, $\estwth_k$, $\sigma_k$, and $\lambda_j$, $\forall j\in\mathcal I$, are uniformly ultimately bounded.

$
\Omega_{NN}

\norm{\mv x_d}\le\overline x_d,

$

\textit{Step 2: Boundedness of the Inner-Layer Variables.}

The remaining weights $[\estwth_i]_{i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}}$ and the Lagrange multipliers $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}}$ are analyzed recursively from $k-1$th layer to the input layer ($i=0$). 
Specifically, for an arbitrary layer index $i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}$, we assume that the subsequent weights $\estwth_{i+1},\cdots,\estwth_k$ have already been shown to be bounded, and then prove the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$.

Consider the following Lyapunov function
$
    V_{i}(\mv{z}_i)
    :=
    \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\estwth_i^\top\estwth_i
    +
    \tfrac{1}{\beta_i^\theta}(\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o)^2
    ,
$
where 
$
    \mv{z}_i:=
    \left(
        \estwth_i^\top,
        \sigma_i
    \right)^\top
    ,
$
and $\sigma_i^o\in\R_{>0}$ will be determined in the subsequent analysis.
Taking time derivative of $V_i$ yields
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vi:dot:1} 
    \ddtt V_i
    \le&
        -\sigma_i \norm{\estwth_i}^2
        -
        \estwth_i^\top
        {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \mv{e}
    \\ \nonumber
    &
        -
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j 
        \estwth_i^\top
        {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\mv{u}}
        +
        2(\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o) c_i^{\theta}(\estwth_i)
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
        -\sigma_i \norm{\estwth_i}^2
        +
        (\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o) 
        \left(
            \norm{\estwth_i}^2
            -
            \overline{\theta}_i^2
        \right)
    \\ \nonumber
    &
        -
        \estwth_i^\top
        \underbrace{
        \left(
            {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
            \mm{P}
            \left(
                \mv{e}
                +
                \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
                \lambda_j 
                \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\mv{u}}
            \right)
        \right)
        }_{=: \gamma_i}
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
        -\sigma_i^o \norm{\estwth_i}^2
        -\sigma_i \overline{\theta}_i^2
        +
        \norm{\estwth_i}
        \norm{\gamma_i}
        +
        \sigma_i^o \overline{\theta}_i^2
    .
\end{align}

For further analysis, the boundedness of $\gamma_i$ is analyzed.
According to the DNN gradient in \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}, $\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}$ is bounded, since $\estwth_{i+1},\cdots,\estwth_k$ are bounded by assumption.
\cred{
The remaining term $\mv{e},\estwth_k,[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ are bounded according to the analysis in Step 1.
The boundedness of $\pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\mv{u}}$ follows from the regularity assumptions on $c_j^u(\cdot)$ and the control input $\mv{u}$.
}
Therefore, $\gamma_i$ is bounded by a positive constant $\overline{\gamma}_i\in\R_{>0}$.

Then, by applying the Young's inequality to the third term in the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:Vi:dot:1}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vi:dot:2}
    \ddtt V_i
    \le&
        -\left(\sigma_i^o
        -
        \tfrac{\overline{\gamma}_i^2}{2}\right)
        \norm{\estwth_i}^2
        -
        \sigma_i \overline{\theta}_i^2
        +
        \underbrace{
        \tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\gamma}_i^2
        +
        \sigma_i^o \overline{\theta}_i^2
        }_{=: \delta_i \in \R_{>0}}
    .
\end{align}
Similarly to $\ddtt V_k$, $\ddtt V_i$ is negative outside the compact set defined by 
$
    \left\{
        \left(
            \estwth_i^\top
            ,
            \sigma_i
        \right)^\top
        :
        -
        \left(
            \sigma_i^o
            -
            \tfrac{\overline{\gamma}_i^2}{2}
        \right)
        \norm{\estwth_i}^2
        -
        \overline{\theta}_i^2
        \norm{\sigma_i}_1
        +
        \delta_i
        \le
        0
    \right\}
    , 
$
which implies the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$.

By recursively applying the above analysis from $i=k-1$ to $i=0$, the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$ for all $i\in\{1,\cdots,k-1\}$ can be established, which concludes the proof.

\end{proof}

Additionally, we introduce the following remarks regarding the tuning of CONAC's parameters and the initialization of DNN weights, which are crucial for achieving desirable performance in practice.

\begin{remark}[Tuning of Adaptation Gain]
The adaptation gain $\alpha$ determines the update speed of the DNN weights.
A large $\alpha$ may improve the initial learning response but can also amplify discretization effects and induce oscillatory behavior in practical sampled-data implementation.
Conversely, an excessively small $\alpha$ may lead to slow adaptation and poor transient tracking performance.
Therefore, $\alpha$ should be selected to balance learning speed and smooth closed-loop behavior.
The effect of $\alpha$ is evaluated in the simulation study in Section~\ref{sec:val:sub:sim}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Tuning of Multiplier Update Rates]
The update rates $\beta_i^\theta$ and $\beta_j^u$ determine how rapidly the Lagrange multipliers respond to weight and input constraint violations.
Larger values accelerate the enforcement of the corresponding constraints by increasing the multiplier response when a violation occurs.
However, excessively large update rates may introduce abrupt changes in the adaptation law, leading to chattering or high-frequency oscillations in the control input.
Thus, these rates should be tuned to balance prompt constraint enforcement and smooth control action.
Their effects are evaluated in the simulation study in Section~\ref{sec:val:sub:sim}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Initialization of DNN Weights]
The initialization of DNN weights can significantly affect the initial tracking performance and the subsequent learning behavior.
A common choice is to initialize the weights with small random values to avoid large initial control inputs.
However, if the system is initialized near an unstable operating region, small random weights may lead to slow learning and poor initial tracking performance.
Therefore, it is preferable to initialize the system in a sufficiently stable region and apply a conservative reference trajectory during the initial warm-up phase.
This strategy prevents abrupt transient behavior and allows the DNN to learn the system dynamics more safely.
The initialization strategy is demonstrated in the real-time experiment in Section~\ref{sec:val:sub:exp}.
\end{remark}


\section{Tuning and Initialization of CONAC's Parameters} \label{sec:tuning}

In this section, we provide guidelines for tuning the parameters of the proposed CONAC, including the adaptation gain $\alpha$ and the update rates of Lagrange multipliers $\beta_i^{\theta}$ and $\beta_j^{u}$, as well as the initialization of NN weights.

\subsection{Tuning of Adaptation Gain and Update Rates of Lagrange Multipliers} \label{sec:tuning:sub:tuning}

The adaptation gain $\alpha$ dominates the adaptation speed of $\estwth$.
Hence, $\alpha$ should not be too large to avoid instability induced by discrete implementation, and not be too small to avoid slow convergence of $\estwth$.
Many studies in the NAC literature tend to employ a large $\alpha$ to demonstrate rapid initial tracking performance.
However, from a practical standpoint, it is much more appropriate and realistic to tune the gain moderately.
\cred{
yielding a gradual and smooth improvement in tracking performance by learning.
The effect of $\alpha$ will be evaluated in the simulation study in Section \ref{sec:val:sub:sim}.
}

The update rates of Lagrange multipliers, $\beta_i^{\theta}$ and $\beta_j^{u}$, determine the speed of addressing constraint violations.
Specifically, larger values of these rates accelerate the growth of the multipliers when a violation occurs, thereby penalizing and mitigating the violation more rapidly.
However, an excessively large update rate may lead to abrupt changes in the adaptation process, which can induce severe chattering or undesirable high-frequency oscillations in the control input.
\cred{
Therefore, these rates must be carefully tuned to strike a proper balance between prompt constraint enforcement and smooth control action.
The effect of the rates also will be evaluated in the simulation study in Section \ref{sec:val:sub:sim}.
}

\subsection{Initialization of NN Weights} \label{sec:tuning:sub:init}

The initialization of NN weights can significantly influence the initial tracking performance and the convergence behavior of the adaptation process.
A common practice is to initialize the weights with small random values, which can prevent large initial control inputs that may destabilize the system.
However, if the system is initialized near to unstable equilibrium points, small random weights may lead to slow learning to stabilize the system, resulting in poor initial tracking performance and potential safety issues.
To mitigate this issue, it is advisable to initialize the system in a sufficiently stable region of the state space, and apply a conservative reference trajectory during the initial warm-up phase. 
This gentle excitation prevents abrupt transient behaviors and allows the neural network sufficient time to learn the system dynamics safely.
This initialization strategy will be demonstrated in the real-time experiment in Section \ref{sec:val:sub:sim}.

\subsubsection*{Controller 1 \normalfont{(C$_1$)}—\textit{CONAC with a large update rate $\beta_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$, for the Lagrange Multipliers}}

To handle input saturation, the proposed CONAC was implemented with the input bound constraints $c_{\overline\tau_2}$ and $c_{\underline\tau_2}$ \eqref{eq:cstr:input:bound} for the second link, and the input norm constraint $c_{\rbu}$ \eqref{eq:cstr:input:norm}, as follows:
\begin{equation}
    c_{\overline\tau_2}     
    =
    \tau_2-\overline\tau_2
    % \le 0
    ,
    \;
    c_{\underline\tau_2} 
    =
    \underline\tau_2-\tau_2
    % \le 0
    ,
    \;
    c_{\rbu}
    =
    \tfrac{1}{2}
    \left(
        \norm{\rbu}-\overline\tau
    \right)^2 
    % <0
    ,
    \label{eq:sim:cstr:CONAC}
\end{equation}
where $\overline\tau_2=-\underline{\tau}_2=3.5$, and $\overline\tau=11$.
Therefore, controller (C$_1$) utilizes the full capability of the actuator, see \eqref{eq:sat:tau2} and \eqref{eq:sat:norm}.
The weight norm constraints $c_{\theta_i}(\cdot),\ \forall i\in\{0,\cdots,k\}$, defined in \eqref{eq:cstr:weight:ball}, were also incorporated into the CONAC framework with $\overline\theta_0=\overline\theta_1=\overline\theta_2=6$.
As described in \eqref{eq:control:est}, the control input (torque) corresponds to the output of the DNN, and the adaptation law is defined in \eqref{eq:adaptation law}.

The update rates of the Lagrange multipliers for the weight norm constraints were set to $\beta_{\theta_i}=1,\ \forall i\in\{0,\cdots,k\}$.
For the input constraints, large update rates were used: $\beta_{\overline{\tau}_2} = \beta_{\underline{\tau}_2} = 100$ and $\beta_{\rbu} = 10$, to investigate the effect of large update rates.

% ------------------------------------
% Controllers: CONAC with small beta
% ------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Controller 2 \normalfont{(C$_2$)}—\textit{CONAC with a small update rate $\beta_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$, for the Lagrange Multipliers}}

The proposed CONAC was implemented with the same input constraints as in (C$_1$), but with update rates for the input constraints reduced by a factor of $100$: $\beta_{\overline{\tau}_2}=\beta_{\underline{\tau}_2}=1$ and $\beta_{\rbu}=0.1$.
All other properties and parameters were kept identical to those of (C$_1$).

\subsubsection*{Controller 3 \normalfont{(C$_3$)}—\textit{CONAC without input constraints}}

The third controller was identical to (C$_1$) and (C$_2$), except that the input constraints were removed to highlight the constraint-handling capability of the proposed CONAC.
In this case, the update rates of the Lagrange multipliers associated with the input constraints, \ie $\beta_{\overline{\tau}_2},\beta_{\underline{\tau}_2}$ and $\beta_{\rbu}$, were set to zero, such that the corresponding Lagrange multipliers $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\{\overline{\tau}_2,\underline{\tau}_2,\rbu\}}$ were not updated, see \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}.
All other properties and parameters were kept the same as in (C$_1$) and (C$_2$).

\subsubsection*{Controller 4 \normalfont{(C$_4$)}—\textit{NAC with auxiliary system (conventional method)}}

As a baseline for comparison, the auxiliary system-based approach from \cite{Esfandiari:2014aa, Esfandiari:2015aa} was implemented.
% in place of the proposed CONAC for handling input constraints.
The auxiliary system is defined by $\ddtt{\mv\zeta} = \mm{A}_\zeta \mv{\zeta} + \mm{B}_\zeta \Delta\rbu$, with initial condition $\mv{\zeta}\vert_{t=0} = \mv{0}_{2\times1}$, where $\mv{\zeta}\in\R^2$ represents the auxiliary state variables, and $\mm{A}_\zeta\in\R_{<0}^{2\times 2}$ and $\mm{B}_\zeta\in\R^{2\times 2}$ are user-designed matrices.
The auxiliary state $\mv{\zeta}$ is driven by the overflow amount of the control input $\Delta\rbu$, where each element is defined as $\Delta\tau_{i} = \tau_{i}-\max(\underline\tau_i,\min(\overline\tau_i,\tau_i)),\ \forall i\in\{1,2\}$.

This auxiliary state was incorporated into the adaptation law by replacing the filtered tracking error $\fe$ with $\fe+\mv{\zeta}$.
As a result, the weights were adapted to minimize both the filtered tracking error and the auxiliary state, thereby mitigating saturation in each control input.

The auxiliary system matrices were chosen as $\mm{A}_\zeta=\mydiag(-10,-10)$ and $\mm{B}_\zeta=\mydiag(10^3,10^3)$, 
to balance responsiveness to input overflow with the convergence speed of the auxiliary dynamics.
Since the auxiliary system only accounts for element-wise input saturation—corresponding to the input bound constraint \eqref{eq:cstr:input:bound}—the upper bound of the first actuator torque $\overline{\tau}_1=-\underline{\tau}_1$ was set to $10.428$ to approximate the input norm constraint $\overline{\tau} = 11$, given $\overline{\tau}_1^2 + \overline{\tau}_2^2 = \overline{\tau}^2$.

All other properties and parameters were kept the same as in (C$_1$), (C$_2$), and (C$_3$), except that the weight boundedness was handled using the projection operator used in \cite{Patil:2022aa}.

The input vector to the DNNs was defined as ${\q}_n=\left(\q^\top,\dq^\top,\fe^\top,1\right)^\top\in\R^{7}$, where the scalar $1$ was included to account for the bias term in the weight matrix.
The adaptation gain was set to $\alpha = 0.2$, and the filtering matrix in \eqref{eq:err:filtered} was chosen as $\mm{\Lambda}=\mydiag(5,15)$.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive controller (CONAC) for unknown Euler-Lagrange systems, addressing both weight norm and convex input constraints within a unified optimization framework. 
The stability of CONAC was analyzed using Lyapunov theory, demonstrating bounded tracking errors and weight estimation under real-time adaptation.

CONAC effectively incorporated both convex input constraints and weight norm constraints, ensuring that actuator limitations and neural network weights remained within predefined bounds. 
By embedding these constraints into the optimization process, CONAC ensured weight convergence in accordance with the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, thereby ensuring both stability and optimality.

Real-time implementation validated the superior performance of CONAC compared to a conventional method. 
CONAC successfully handled complex input constraints while rigorously managing weight norms, resulting in improved tracking accuracy and stable behavior without significant oscillations.

Future work may extend this approach to include constraints on both control inputs and system states, further enhancing the flexibility and robustness of neuro-adaptive control systems within constrained optimization frameworks.

Currently, he serves as an Assistant Professor at the Cho Chun Shik Graduate School of Mobility at KAIST, where he is also the Director of the Mobility Intelligence and Control Laboratory.
