%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
  This study presents a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) for a class of unknown multi-input-multi-output (MIMO) systems subject to multiple convex input constraints. 
  A deep neural network (DNN) is employed to approximate the ideal control law while handling multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework.
  The adaptive variables including the DNN weights and Lagrange multipliers are updated through adaptation laws derived from the formulated constrained optimization problem, yielding Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)-like first-order optimality conditions at equilibrium.
  The controller's stability is rigorously analyzed using Lyapunov theory, guaranteeing uniformly ultimately bounded tracking errors, and adaptive variables.
  The proposed controller is compared with existing methods in real-time implementation on a 2-degree-of-freedom (DOF) robotic manipulator, highlighting superior capability in handling input saturation and feasibility in real-time applications.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
    Neuro-adaptive control, constrained optimization, deep neural network,  multiple convex input constraints.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{rom} a practical perspective, many engineering systems are constrained by various safety requirements, actuator limitations, and physical restrictions \cite{Ji:2025aa}.
These constraints often manifest as convex input constraints, which can be in the form of nonlinear constraints rather than simple box constraints that limit each input independently, \ie $\underline{u}_i\le u_i\le\overline{u}_i$, $\forall i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$, where $\underline{u}_i,\overline{u}_i\in\R$ denote the lower and upper bounds of the $i$th input $u_i$, respectively.
For example, under road friction limits, the admissible global force and moment of vehicles can be characterized by weighted norm bound, which yields convex ellipsoidal constraint \cite{Chen:2024aa}.
In motor drive systems, the feasible voltage region is often represented by circular \cite{Ryu:2025aa} or hexagonal sets \cite{Monzen:2026aa}.
Furthermore, admissible input sets may be determined by multiple constraints that must hold simultaneously, such as component-wise bounds combined with norm constraints, which can be represented as the intersection of multiple convex sets.
Therefore, addressing these multiple convex input constraints is crucial for ensuring the safety and reliability of control systems in real-world applications.

In parallel, engineering systems often involve uncertain or even unknown dynamics due to modeling errors, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.
Although adaptive control provides a systematic mechanism for online adaptation of controllers \cite{Ioannou:2006aa}, conventional formulations often rely on structural assumption on the uncertain or unknown dynamics, which may not be available in practice.
Neuro-adaptive control (NAC) alleviates this assumption by using neural networks (NNs) to approximate the dynamics in a structured and parameterized representation \cite{Farrell:2006aa,Patil:2022aa}.
Like adaptive control, NAC methods ensure closed-loop stability using Lyapunov theory, and the NN weights are adapted online without prior knowledge of the system.
The NAC methods have shown promising results in various real-world applications, including vehicle control \cite{You:2023aa}, and flexible robotic manipulators \cite{Gao:2026aa}.

Because multiple convex input constraints and uncertain or unknown dynamics often coexist in real-world applications, incorporating multiple input constraint handling into NAC is essential.
This motivates a review of existing input constraint handling methods in NAC and related constrained control frameworks.

\subsubsection{Structural Methods for Input Constraint Handling in NAC}

Since the NAC framework is based on Lyapunov theory, structural methods have been widely studied for input constraint handling.
In \cite{Ji:2025aa}, Ji \etal categorized the literature on input constraint handling into two strategies: anti-saturation designs and direct design.
We review these strategies in the context of NAC.

In anti-saturation designs, a virtual auxiliary system is mainly constructed to compensate for the effects of input saturation \cite{Gao:2017aa,Ma:2025aa,Gao:2026aa,Yang:2021aa,Jia:2020aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa,He:2016aa,He:2019aa,Zhao:2016aa,Peng:2020aa}.
Owing to the high compatibility of the auxiliary system design, the auxiliary system has been adopted in various control design.
For example, auxiliary systems have been incorporated into backstepping-based NAC \cite{Gao:2017aa} and sliding mode control (SMC)-based NAC \cite{Ma:2025aa}, to mitigate the effects of input saturation.
Furthermore, the auxiliary systems have been integrated into reinforcement learning (RL)-based NAC designs \cite{Gao:2026aa} and barrier Lyapunov function (BLF)-based NAC designs \cite{Yang:2021aa}.
To achieve finite-time or fixed-time convergence, novel finite-time \cite{Jia:2020aa} or fixed-time \cite{Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa} auxiliary systems have been proposed.

Generally, when the control input reaches the saturation limit, the auxiliary system is excited, and the auxiliary state is fed back into the control law as additional compensation terms
However, from a learning perspective, if the auxiliary state is not incorporated into the feedback error used in the adaptation law, the NN weights may generate excessive control input to achieve the desired tracking performance, since the saturation effect is not considered in the adaptation process.
Therefore, the auxiliary state is often considered in the adaptation law  \cite{Gao:2017aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa}.
This ensures that the NN weights adapt not only for approximating the ideal control law but also for compensating the effect of input saturation.

On the other hand, in direct design, the saturation effects are directly incorporated into the controller design and stability analysis.
Typically, the saturation function is approximated by a smooth function, such as $\tanh(\cdot)$, and mean-value theorem is used to transform it into affine form \cite{Wang:2013aa,Cui:2014aa,Li:2024aa}.
However, as reported in \cite{Min:2021aa}, the smooth approximation of the saturation function may introduce conservative behavior and performance degradation, especially when the control input is close to the saturation limit, compared to the auxiliary system-based anti-saturation design.

Despite their effectiveness, aforementioned structural methods are limited to simple box input constraints, such as symmetric or asymmetric bounds on each input.
Thus, extending them to admissible input sets described by multiple convex constraints is not straightforward.

\subsubsection{Optimization-Based Methods for Input Constraint Handling}

Besides structural methods, optimization-based methods have been studied for constraints handling across a broad range of control design frameworks.
These methods compute the control input by solving constrained optimization problems at each time instant, as in model predictive control (MPC) \cite{Greune:2011aa,Bemporad:1999aa,Berberich:2025aa,Draeger:1995aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa} and control barrier function (CBF)-based quadratic programming (QP) filters \cite{Ames:2019aa,Ko:2025aa,Sweatland:2025aa}. 
In these methods, the constrained control problem is explicitly formulated as a constrained optimization problem.
Therefore, complex nonlinear (even non-convex) input constraints can be systematically incorporated into the control design, which is a significant advantage over structural methods.

% \cite{Greune:2011aa,Bemporad:1999aa,Berberich:2025aa,Draeger:1995aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa}, \cite{Ames:2019aa,Ko:2025aa,Sweatland:2025aa}

% MPC 
% Bemporad:1999aa,Berberich:2025aa,Draeger:1995aa,Chen:2024aa,Cetin:2019aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa

However, optimization-based methods require additional conditions to ensure closed-loop stability.
Representatively, in MPC design, the recursive feasibility, computational tractability, and sufficiently long prediction horizons are essential \cite{Greune:2011aa}.
Since the model accuracy is most critical across these conditions, the uncertain or unknown dynamics have motivated robust \cite{Bemporad:1999aa}, data-driven \cite{Berberich:2025aa}, and learning-based MPC \cite{Draeger:1995aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa}.
For CBF-QP filters, neuro-adaptive system identifiers have been employed to accurately impose the CBF constraint using approximated system dynamics \cite{Sweatland:2025aa}.
Additionally, for computational tractability, some studies have proposed learning the optimal control policy offline using NNs \cite{Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa}.
Although these methods can provide stability guarantees under the stated conditions, the guarantees are typically obtained through feasibility, prediction, or filtering mechanisms that depend on model accuracy, uncertainty descriptions, data quality, or offline training coverage.

\subsection{Motivation and Proposed Approach}

Both structural and optimization-based methods are effective for input constraint handling, but they have complementary limitations, as summarized in Table~\ref{table:intro:comparison}.
Structural methods offer Lyapunov-based stability guarantees and solver-free computation, but they are limited in the class of constraints they can accommodate.
Optimization-based methods can handle complex constraints, but their closed-loop stability guarantees are often tied to additional conditions.

These observations motivate a unified NAC framework in which more complex input constraints are incorporated directly into the online adaptation law, as presented in \cite{Ryu:2025aa}.
In the proposed CONAC framework, the NAC problem is formulated as a constrained optimization problem in which multiple convex input constraints are imposed as inequality constraints.
The constrained optimization formulation is not used to solve an online control allocation problem at each time instant; instead, it is used to derive continuous-time adaptation laws for the adaptive variables.
Therefore, the proposed framework retains the Lyapunov-friendly structure of NAC while incorporating the constraint-handling capability of optimization-based methods.

\section{Problem Formulation} \label{sec:problem:formulation}

We consider an unknown nonlinear system modeled as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sys}
  \ddtt\mv{x} 
  = 
  \mv{f}(\mv{x}) 
  + 
  \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u})
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{x},\mv{u}\in\R^n$ denote state and control input vectors, respectively, and $\mv{f}:\R^n\to\R^n$ and $\mm{B}:\R^n\to\R^{n\times n}$ denote unknown system function and control effectiveness matrix, which are locally Lipschitz continuous, respectively.
The saturation function is denoted by $\mysat:\R^n\to\Omega_u$, where $\Omega_u\subset\R^n$ is the admissible input set.
% The compact set $\Omega_x$ denotes a approximation region where NN will approximate with minimum bounded approximation error, later.

The system \eqref{eq:sys} is subject to the following assumptions.

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound}
  The control effectiveness matrix $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ is nonsingular and bounded as   
  $
    \underline{b}\mm{I}_n
    \preceq 
    \mm{B}(\mv{x})
    \preceq 
    \bar{b}\mm{I}_n
    ,
  $
  where $\underline{b},\bar{b}\in\R_{>0}$ are unknown positive constants.
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound:grad}
  \cite[Assumption 1(b)]{Boulkroune:2010aa}
  There exists an unknown positive function $\beta(\mv{x}):\R^n\to\R_{>0}$ such that $\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{v}^\top\left(\ddtt\mm{B}(\mv{x})^{-1}\right)\mv{v} \le \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{v}}^2$, $\forall \mv{v}\in\R^n$ and $\forall \mv{x}\in\R^n$.
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:sat}
    The admissible input set $\Omega_u$ is defined by smooth convex constraint functions $c_j^u(\mv u)$, $\forall j\in\mathcal I$, where $\mathcal{I}$ denotes the input constraint index set, as
    $
        \Omega_u
        =
        \bigcap_{j\in\mathcal I}
        \{\mv u\in\R^n:c_j^u(\mv u)\le0\}
        .
    $
    The origin is contained in its interior, \ie $c_j^u(\mv 0) \le -\underline{c}_j^u <0$, $\forall j\in\mathcal I$, for known $\underline{c}_j^u\in\R_{>0}$.
  % The saturation function $\mysat(\cdot)$ satisfies $\mysat(\mv u)=\mv u$ for $\mv u\in\Omega_u$ and, for $\mv u\notin\Omega_u$, $\mysat(\mv u)$ lies on the ray from the origin through $\mv u$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:saturation}.
    Moreover, there exists known $\underline u\in\R_{>0}$ such that
    $
        \inf_{\mv{u}\notin\Omega_u}
        \left(
            \norm{\mysat(\mv u)}
        \right)
        =
        \underline u
        .
    $
\end{assum}

By Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}, the saturation function $\mysat(\mv{u})$ can represent any combination of convex input constraints, rather than simple box constraints which are conventionally considered in the NAC literature \cite{Gao:2017aa}, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:saturation}.

\begin{assum} \label{assum:xd}
    The desired trajectory $\mv{x}_d(t)$ is sufficiently smooth and $\mv{x}_d,\ddtt\mv{x}_d$ are bounded.
    % The desired trajectory $\mv{x}_d(t)$ is sufficiently smooth and bounded, such that $\mv{x}_d\in\Omega_{x_d}:=\left\{\mv{x}_d\in\R^n : \norm{\mv{x}_d}\le \overline{x}_d\right\}$ and $\ddtt\mv{x}_d$ is bounded in a compact set $\Omega_{dx_d}$, as well.
\end{assum}

The control objective is to design a NAC for the unknown system \eqref{eq:sys} such that $\mv{x}(t)$ tracks the desired trajectory $\mv{x}_d(t)$, the multiple convex input constraints defined by $\mysat(\cdot)$ are satisfied, and closed-loop stability is guaranteed.

We review the DNN structure and its approximation capability, which are essential for the subsequent controller design and stability analysis.
According to the universal approximation property \cite{Patil:2022aa}, the DNNs $\NN: \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1} \times \mathbb{R}^{\Xi} \to \mathbb{R}^{l_k+1}$ can be used to approximate any smooth unknown nonlinear functions $\mv{g}:\R^{l_0+1}\to\R^{l_{k+1}}$.
Specifically, there exists a DNN such that 
\begin{equation}
  \sup_{\NNin\in\Omega_{\NNin}}
  \norm{
    \mv{g}(\NNin) - \NN(\NNin;\wth^*)
  }
  =
  \overline{\epsilon}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\NNin \in \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ is the DNN input vector, $\Omega_{\NNin} \subset \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ is a compact set, and $\idealwth \in \mathbb{R}^{\Xi}$ denotes the ideal DNN weights that minimize the approximation error $\overline{\epsilon} > 0$.
The ideal DNN weights $\idealwth$ are assumed to be bounded.
The DNN $\NN(\NNin;\wth)=\NN_k$ can be recursively defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dnn:def}
  \NN_i :=
  \begin{cases}
    \wV_i^\top \act_i(\NN_{i-1}), 
    &
    i\in\{1,\dots ,k\},
    \\
    \wV_0^\top \NNin,
    &
    i=0
    ,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\wV_i\in\R^{(l_i+1)\times l_{i+1}}$ and $\act_i: \R^{l_i}\to\R^{l_i+1}$ represent the weight matrix and the activation function of the $i$th layer for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$, respectively, and $\mathcal{K}:=\{0,1,\cdots,k\}$ denotes the index set of layers.
The DNN $\NN(\NNin;\wth)$ has $k+1$ layers, where the input and output layers are indexed by $i=0$ and $i=k$, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:DNN} as an example.
The activation function is defined as $\act_i(\mv{z}) = \left(\tanh(z_1),\cdots,\tanh(z_{l_i}),1\right)^\top,$ for some $\mv{z}\in\R^{l_i}$.
We simplify notations by vectorizing all weight matrices as 
\begin{equation*}
  \wth_i 
  :=
  \myvec(\wV_i)
  \in\R^{(l_i+1)l_{i+1}}
  ,\ 
  \forall 
  i\in\mathcal{K}
  ,
\end{equation*}
and, finally, stacking them as $\wth := \left[\wth_i\right]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}\in\R^\Xi$, where $\Xi := \sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}} (l_i+1)l_{i+1}$.
Additionally, there exist maximum norm of each layer's weights $\overline{\theta}^*_i\in\R_{>0}$, such that $\norm{\idealwth_i}\le\overline{\theta}^*_i$, $\forall i\in\mathcal{K}$.

The gradient of $ \NN(\NNin;\wth)$ with respect to $\wth$ can be obtained using Proposition~\ref{propsit:kron} and the chain rule as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth}=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_k}&
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_{k-1}}&
\cdots &
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_0}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \in\R^{l_{k+1} \times \Xi}
  ,
  \label{eq:gradient:phi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth_i} = 
  \begin{cases}
    % (
      \mm I_{l_{k+1}}
      \otimes 
      \act_{k}^\top  
    % )
    , 
    &
    i=k
    ,
    \\
    \wV_k^\top\act_{k}' 
    (
      \mm I_{l_{k}}
      \otimes  
      \act_{k-1}^\top  
    )
    , 
    & 
    i=k-1
    ,
    \\
    \vdots 
    &
    \vdots 
    \\
    \wV_k^\top\act'_{k} 
    \cdots 
    \wV_c^\top\act_1' 
    (
      \mm I_{l_1}
      \otimes 
      \NNin^\top  
    )
    , 
    &
    i = 0
    ,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:NN:grad}
\end{equation}
and $\act_i:= \act_i(\NN_{i-1})$ and $\act_i':= \ddtfrac{\act_i}{\NN_{i-1}}$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}$.

\subsection{Neuro-Adaptive Control Design} \label{sec:main:result:sub:ctrl:design}

We define the tracking error as $\mv{e} = \mv{x} - \mv{x}_d$, and derive the error dynamics from \eqref{eq:sys} as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:error:dyn}
    \ddtt\mv{e}
    =
    \mv{f}(\mv{x}) + \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u}) - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    .
\end{equation}
Let us consider Lyapunov function $V_c:=\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}(\mv{x})\mv{e}$.
Taking time derivative of $V_c$ yields
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vc:dot:1} \nonumber
  \ddtt V_c
  =&
  \tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top
  \left(
    \ddtt\mm{B}^{-1}
  \right)
  \mv{e}
  +
  \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
  \left(
    \mv{f} + \mm{B}\mysat(\mv{u}) 
    - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
  \right)
  \\
  \le&
  \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{e}}^2
  \!+\!
  \mv{e}^\top\mv{u}
  \!+\!
  \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
  \left(
    \mv{f} \!-\! \ddtt\mv{x}_d
  \right)
  \!+\!
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta\mv{u}
  ,
\end{align}
where arguments of $\mv{f}(\mv{x})$ and $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ are omitted for brevity and $\Delta\mv{u} := \mysat(\mv{u}) - \mv{u}$ denotes amount of input saturation.

A desired control law $\mv{u}_d$ such that the tracking error $\mv{e}$ is stable in the absence of the input saturation, is selected as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ctrl:ideal}
  \mv{u}_d
  :=
  -
  \mm{P}
  \mv{e}
  - 
  \beta(\mv{x})\mv{e}
  +
  \mm{B}^{-1}
  \left(
    - \mv{f} 
    + \ddtt\mv{x}_d
  \right)
  % +
  % h(\mv{x},\mv{x}_d)
  ,
\end{equation}
where user-defined matrix $\mm{P}=\mm{P}^\top\in\R^{n\times n}$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix satisfying $\underline{p}\mm{I}_n \preceq \mm{P} \preceq \overline{p}\mm{I}_n$ for known positive constants $\underline{p},\overline{p}\in\R_{>0}$.
% The function $h:\R^n\times\R^n\to\R^n$ is correction term to satisfy the ideal control law $\mv{u}^*$ within the input constraints, \ie $\mv{u}^*\in\Omega_u$.

By substituting $\mv{u}_d$ in \eqref{eq:ctrl:ideal} into \eqref{eq:Vc:dot:1}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vc:dot:2} 
  \ddtt V_c
  \le &
  -\mv{e}^\top
  \mm{P}
  \mv{e}
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \left(
    \mv{u} - \mv{u}_d
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta\mv{u}
  \\ \nonumber
  \le & 
  -
  \underline{p}\norm{\mv{e}}^2
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \left(
    \mv{u} - \mv{u}_d       
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  % \left(
    \Delta\mv{u}
%     +
%     h(\mv{x},\mv{x}_d)
  % \right)
  .
\end{align}
Notably, in ideal case, \ie $\mv{u} = \mv{u}_d$ and $\Delta\mv{u} = \mv{0}$, the tracking error $\mv{e}$ is asymptotically exponentially stable with decay rate $\underline{p}\overline{b}$, since 
$
  \tfrac{1}{\overline{b}}
  \ddtt
  \left(
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
  \right)
  \le
  \ddtt V_c 
  \le 
  -\underline{p}\norm{\mv{e}}^2
$.

Since the ideal control law \eqref{eq:ctrl:ideal} contains unknown functions $\beta(\mv{x})$, $\mv{f}$ and $\mm{B}$, we employ the DNN $\NN(\NNin;\wth)$ in \eqref{eq:dnn:def} to approximate as follows:
\begin{equation*}
% \begin{aligned}

  +
  \mv{\epsilon}
  =
  -\mv{e}
  -
  \mm{P}^{-1}
  \left(
  \beta(\mv{x})\mv{e}
  +
  \mm{B}^{-1}
  (
    \mv{f}
    -
    \ddtt \mv{x}_d

  \right)
  ,
% \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $\mv{\epsilon}\in\R^n$ denotes an approximation error vector, such that 
$
\sup_{\NNin\in\Omega_{\NNin}}   
\left( 
  \norm{\mv{\epsilon}}
\right)
=
\overline{\epsilon}
.
$
The DNN input vector is defined as 
$
  \NNin 
  = 
  \left(
    \mv{x}_d^\top, 
    \ddtt{\mv{x}}_d^\top, 
    \mv{x}^\top, 
    1 
  \right)^\top
  \in\R^{3n+1}
$.
Notably, the DNN input vector $\NNin$ can be defined in various ways, as long as it consists of the unknown functions.
Then, we have 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ctrl:NN:ideal}
  \mv{u}_d
  =
  \mm{P}
  \big(
    \NN(\NNin;\idealwth)
    +
    \mv{\epsilon}
  \big)
  .
\end{equation}
The actual control law is designed as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ctrl:NN:actual}
  \mv{u}
  :=
  \mm{P}
  \NN(\NNin;\estwth)
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\estwth\in\R^{\Xi}$ denotes the estimated DNN weights.

By substituting \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:ideal} and \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:actual} into \eqref{eq:Vc:dot:2}, we have
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Vc:dot:3}
  \ddtt V_c
  \le
  - \underline{p}
  \norm{\mv{e}}^2
  +
  \mv{e}^\top\mm{P}
  \left(
    \estNN - \idealNN
    - \mv{\epsilon}
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta\mv{u}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\estNN\!:=\!\NN(\NNin;\estwth)$ and $\idealNN\!:=\!\NN(\NNin;\wth^*)$ are abbreviated.

\subsection{Adaptation Law Derivation} \label{sec:main:result:sub:adap:laws}

% The control objective which minimizes the tracking error $\mv{e}$ while satisfying both weight boundedness and input constraints can be represented as follows:
The control objectives defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem:formulation} can be formulated in a constrained optimization problem, as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:opt:prob} 
  \begin{align} 
    \min_{\estwth} \quad 
    & 
    J(\mv{e};\estwth)
    = 
    \tfrac{1}{2} \mv{e}^\top \mv{e} 
    &
    \label{eq:opt:prob:obj} 
    \\ 
    \text{subject to} \quad  
    & 
    c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i)=\tfrac{1}{2}
    \left(
      \norm{\estwth_i}^2
      -
      \overline{\theta}_i^2 
    \right)
    \le 0, 
    \!
    &
    \!
    \forall i\in\mathcal{K} 
    ,
    \label{eq:opt:prob:const1} 
    \\ 
    & 
    c^{u}_j(\estwth)
    % c_j^u
    % \left(
    %     \mm{P}\NN(\NNin;\estwth)
    % \right) 
    \le 0, 
    &
    \forall j\in\mathcal{I} 
    \label{eq:opt:prob:const2} 
    ,
  \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $J(\mv{e};\estwth)$ is the objective function.
Inequality constraints of weight boundedness $c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i),\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K},$ and input saturation $c^{u}_j(\estwth),\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I},$ are considered.

Specifically, the input constraints used in \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2} are the same convex constraint functions that characterize the admissible input set in Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}.
With a slight abuse of notation, the composed input constraints are denoted by
$c_j^u(\estwth):=c_j^u(\mv u(\estwth))=c_j^u(\mm P\NN(\NNin;\estwth)),\ \forall j\in\mathcal I$, to explicitly represent the dependence of the input constraints on the DNN weights $\estwth$.
Additionally, all active constraints are assumed to satisfy the Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ) to avoid degeneracy \cite[pp. 465--467]{Nocedal:2006aa}.

The Lagrangian function $L:=L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
  L
  = 
  J(\mv{e};\estwth) 
  + 
  \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
  \sigma_{i}
  c^{\theta}_{i}(\estwth_i)
  +
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}
  \lambda_{j}
  c^{u}_{j}(\estwth)
  ,
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ denote the Lagrange multipliers for each weight \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const1} and input constraints \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2}, respectively.
Then, the primal-dual problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:opt:prob:saddle}
  \min_{\estwth}
  \max_{
    [\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
    ,
    [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  } 
  L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})
  .
\end{equation}

The adaptation laws for the adaptive variables, $\estwth$, and $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$, are derived to solve \eqref{eq:opt:prob:saddle} by using a primal-dual gradient update law, \ie 
$
  \ddtt\estwth 
  = 
  -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}
$, $
  \ddtt\sigma_i 
  = 
  \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
  \left(
      \beta_i^\theta 
      \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i}
  \right)
$, and $
  \ddtt\lambda_j 
  = 
  \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
  \left(
    \beta_j^u 
    \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j}
  \right)
$, as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:adap}
  \begin{align}
    \ddtt{\estwth}
    &
    =
    -\alpha 
    \left(
      \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
      +
      \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
      \sigma_i 
      \estwth_i
      +
      \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
      \lambda_j 
      \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\estwth}
    \right),
    \label{eq:adap:th}
    \\
    \ddtt\sigma_i
    & 
    = 
    \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
      \left(
      \beta_i^{\theta}
      c^{\theta}_{i} 
    \right)
    ,
    \quad \forall i\in\mathcal{K},
    \label{eq:adap:sig}
    \\
    \ddtt\lambda_j
    & 
    = 
    \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
      \left(
      \beta_j^{u}
      c^{u}_{j} 
    \right)
    ,
    \quad \forall j\in\mathcal{I},
    \label{eq:adap:lbd}
  \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $\alpha\in\R_{>0}$ denotes the adaptation gain (learning rate) and $\beta_i^{\theta},\beta_j^{u}\in\R_{>0},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K},j\in\mathcal{I}$, denote the update rate of the Lagrange multipliers. 
The Lagrange multipliers associated with inequality constraints are kept non-negative, \ie $\sigma_i,\lambda_j\ge 0$, due to the projection operator $\myproj_{(\cdot)\ge0}(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}, which are presented in \cite[Appendix E, eq. (E.4)]{Krstic:1995aa}.

When a constraint becomes violated, \ie positive constraint violation $c^{\theta}_{i},c^{u}_{j}\ge0$, the corresponding Lagrange multiplier increases, see \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}, to address the violation by updating weight $\estwth$, as shown in \eqref{eq:adap:th}.
Ultimately, the Lagrange multiplier exhibits two distinct behaviors depending on the constraint status: it either decreases to zero (\eg via a projection operator) when the constraint becomes inactive, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j < 0$, or converges to a positive value when the constraint remains binding, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j = 0$, to enforce the limit.

Meanwhile, the adaptation law of $\estwth$ defined in \eqref{eq:adap:th} involves the partial derivative of the error with respect to control input $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$, \ie 
$
    \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
    =
    \big(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        (\pptfrac{\mv{u}}{\estwth})
    \big)
    ^\top \mv{e}
    =
    % \left(
    \big(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        \mm{P}
        (\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth})
    \big)
    % \right)
    ^\top \mv{e}
$. 
Since the objective function depends on $\mv{e}$ of a dynamic system, obtaining $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ is not straightforward. 
Exact sensitivity computation methods, such as the forward sensitivity method \cite{Khalil:2002aa,Ryu:2025ab}, can be used in principle, but they are often computationally demanding for real-time applications with numerous DNN weights.
Therefore, several approximation methods have been proposed \cite{Douratsos:2007aa,Saerens:1991aa}.
In the literature, the authors approximate $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ by taking the sign of each entry of the control effectiveness matrix, \ie
$
  \pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}
  \approx
  \big[
    \mysign
    % \left(
    (
        % \pptfrac{e_i}{u_j}
    % \right)
    b_{i,j}
    )
  \big]_{i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}}
  ,
$
which corresponds to MIT rule in the context of adaptive control.
However, since the entry of the control effectiveness matrix $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ is unknown in this study, we instead approximate $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ by its sign, \ie $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}\approx\mysign(\mm{B}(\mv{x}))=\mm{I}_{n}$, under Assumption~\ref{assum:B:bound}.
Consequently, the adaptation law in \eqref{eq:adap:th} becomes:
\begin{equation}
  \ddtt{\estwth}
  \approx
  -\alpha 
  \left(
    {\pptfrac{\estNN}
    {\estwth}}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
    \sigma_i 
    \estwth_i
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j 
    \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\estwth}
  \right)
  .
  \label{eq:adap:th:approx}
\end{equation}

Additionally, the adaptation law \eqref{eq:adap:th} provides an optimality interpretation at equilibrium.
At an equilibrium of the adaptation law \eqref{eq:adap}, where $\ddtt{\estwth}=0$, $\ddtt\sigma_i=0$ and $\ddtt\lambda_j=0$, the resulting equilibrium conditions correspond to KKT-like conditions, \ie $\pptfrac{L}{\estwth}\approx0$, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j \le 0$, $\sigma_i,\lambda_j \ge 0$, and $\sigma_i c^{\theta}_i=0$, and $\lambda_j c^{u}_j=0,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K},j\in\mathcal{I}$.
Thus, the equilibria characterizes candidates for locally optimal solutions of the formulated constrained adaptation problem.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
  \caption{Proposed CONAC}\label{alg:CONAC}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE \textbf{Initialize:} $\estwth^{(0)}$, $\sigma_i^{(0)},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}$, $\lambda_j^{(0)},\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$
    \FOR{$r = 0 $ to $\infty$}
      \STATE Obtain $\mv{x}^{(r)}$ and $\mv{x}_d^{(r)}$
      \STATE $\mv{e}^{(r)}\leftarrow\mv{x}^{(r)}-\mv{x}_d^{(r)}$

      \STATE \textit{// Neuro-adaptive control law (Section~\ref{sec:main:result:sub:ctrl:design})}

        \STATE $\NNin^{(r)}\leftarrow

          \right)^\top$

        \STATE $\mv{u}^{(r)}\leftarrow\NN(\NNin^{(r)};\estwth^{(r)}) $ based on \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:actual}
        \STATE Apply $\mv{u}^{(r)}$ to the system in \eqref{eq:sys}

\section{Stability Analysis} \label{sec:stability:analysis}

In this section, the stability of the closed-loop system under the proposed CONAC is analyzed.
To facilitate the stability analysis, we introduce the following lemmas.

\begin{lem}[Convexity of input constraints with respect to $\estwth_k$] \label{lem:convexity:input:constraint}
  The input constraint function $c_j^{u}(\estwth_k),\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I},$ is convex with respect to the output layer weights $\estwth_k$, resulting in inequality $c_j^{u}(\estwth_k) - \pptfrac{c_j^{u}}{\estwth_k}^\top \estwth_k \le c_j^{u}(\estwth)\big|_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  % The dependence of the preceding layers and $\NNin$ on the output layer appears through the activation vector $\act_k$.
  % According to the DNN gradient in \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}, we have
  According to Proposition~\ref{propsit:kron}, the output of the DNN $\estNN$ depends linearly on the output layer weights $\estwth_k$ as follows:
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:NN:linear}
    \estNN
    =
    \estwV_k^\top
    \estact_k
    = 
    \left(
      \mm{I}_{l_{k+1}}
      \!
      \otimes
      \! 
      \estact_k^\top
    \right)
    \myvec(\estwV_k)
    = 
    \left(
      \mm{I}_{l_{k+1}} 
      \!
      \otimes 
      \!
      \estact_k^\top
    \right)
    \estwth_k
    .
  \end{equation}
  This implies that the control input $\mv{u}=\mm{P}\estNN$ depends linearly on $\estwth_k$.
  Therefore, the convexity of $c_j^u(\estwth),\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$, with respect to $\mv{u}$ is preserved with respect to $\estwth_k$, due to the linear dependence of $\mv{u}$ on $\estwth_k$.
  In addition, the convexity of $c_j^u(\estwth)$ with respect to $\estwth_k$ implies the desired inequality, concluding the proof.
  % , where $c_j^u(\mv{0})$ denotes the value at $\estwth_k=\mv{0}$
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Boundedness of $\Delta\mv{u}$ with respect to $\estwth_k$] \label{lem:delta:u:bound}
  The saturated amount of control input $\Delta\mv{u}$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as $\norm{\Delta\mv{u}}\le \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\act_k(\cdot)$ employs the bounded activation function $\vert\tanh(\cdot)\vert < 1$, we have 
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\act_k(\cdot)} \le \sqrt{l_k+1}
    ,
  \end{equation}
  for any output of the preceding layers and $\NNin$.
  Hence, invoking the relation in \eqref{eq:NN:linear}, the control input $\mv{u}=\mm{P}\estNN$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as follows:
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:NN:bound}
    \norm{\mv{u}} 
    \le
    \overline{p}
    \norm{\estNN}
    \le
    \overline{p}
    \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
    .   
  \end{equation}

  In addition, since there exists $\underline{u}>0$ such that $\inf_{\mv u\notin\Omega_u}\allowbreak(\norm{\mysat(\mv u)})=\underline{u}$ by Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}, we have
  $
    \norm{\Delta\mv u}
    \le
    \norm{\mv u}-\underline{u}
    ,
  $ 
  while $\Delta\mv u=\mv 0$ when $\mv u\in\Omega_u$.
  Thus, we have
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\Delta\mv{u}}
    \le
    \max
    \left\{
      \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
      -
      \underline{u}
      ,
      0
    \right\}
    \le
    \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
    ,  
  \end{equation}
  which concludes the proof.    
\end{proof}

\begin{lem} \label{lem:proj:ineq}
  For any $a,b\in\R_{\ge0}$ and $c\in\R_{>0}$, the following inequality holds:
  \begin{equation}
    (a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) \le (a-b) c.
  \end{equation}
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  The inequality can be verified by considering the two cases of $a>0$ and $a=0$, as follows:
  \begin{equation*}
    (a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) 
    =
    \begin{cases}
      (a-b) c, & \text{if } a>0,
      \\
      0, & \text{if } a=0,
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation*}
  This implies that $(a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) \le (a-b) c$ always holds, which concludes the proof.
\end{proof}

The following theorem establishes the UUB of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{I}$ and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:stability}
  Consider the closed-loop system consisting of \eqref{eq:sys}, the control law \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:actual}, and the adaptation laws \eqref{eq:adap}.
  Suppose that Assumptions~\ref{assum:B:bound}--\ref{assum:sat} hold and the initial condition belongs to the compact invariant set 
  $
  \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal K}
  \mathcal S_i(\overline V_i)
  $ 
  constructed in \eqref{eq:Sk:def} and \eqref{eq:Si:def}.
  Then, the tracking error $\mv e$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{K},$ and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I},$ are uniformly ultimately bounded.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

The proof is divided into two steps.
We first analyze the UBB of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the adaptive variables of output layer, $\estwth_k$, and $\sigma_k$, and the Lagrange multipliers associated with the input constraints $\lambda_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$.
This step provides the key boundedness result for the tracking error and the controller output.
In the following step, analysis of the remaining adaptive variables of inner layer, $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{k\}$, follows.

\hfill

\textit{Step~1: Boundedness of the tracking error, output layer and input constraint adaptive variables.}

Consider the following Lyapunov function 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Vk:def}
  V_{k}(\mv{z}_k)
  :=
  V_c
  +
  \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\estwth_k^\top\estwth_k
  +
  \tfrac{1}{\beta_k^\theta}(\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o)^2
  +
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  \tfrac{1}{2\beta_j^{u}}\lambda_j^2
  ,
\end{equation}
where 
$
  \mv{z}_k
  :=
  \left(
    \mv{e}^\top,
    \estwth_k^\top,
    \sigma_k,
    \left[
      \lambda_j
    \right]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}^\top
  \right)^\top
$ and $\sigma_k^o\in\R_{>0}$ will be determined later.
The Lyapunov function $V_k:=V_k(\mv{z}_k)$ is lower bounded and radially unbounded with respect to $\mv{z}_k$.
In the following analysis, we consider the case where the output layer weight constraint is violated, \ie $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)>0$.
If this condition does not hold, then $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)\le0$ implies $\norm{\estwth_k}\le\overline{\theta}_k$, and the boundedness of $\estwth_k$ follows directly.

The time derivative of $V_{k}$ can be analyzed as follows:
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:1} \nonumber
  \ddtt V_{k}
  \le&
  -\underline{p}
  \norm{\mv{e}}^2
  +
  \mv{e}^\top\mm{P}
  \left(
    \estNN - \idealNN
    - \mv{\epsilon}
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta \mv{u}
  \\ \nonumber
  &
  -
  \underbrace{
    \estwth_k^\top
    \pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_k}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
  }_{
    = 
    \estNN^\top\mm{P}\mv{e}
    ,\ \text{see, \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}, \eqref{eq:NN:linear}}
  }
  -
  \sigma_k
  \estwth_k^\top
  \estwth_k
  -    
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  \lambda_j
  \estwth_k^\top
  \pptfrac{c^{u}_j}{\estwth_k}
  \\ % \nonumber
  &
  +
  2(\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o) c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)
  +
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  \lambda_j c^{u}_j(\estwth)
  \\ \nonumber
  \le&
  -\underline{p}
  \norm{\mv{e}}^2
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \mm{P}
  \left(
    - \idealNN
    - \mv{\epsilon}
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta \mv{u}
  -
  \sigma_k \norm{\estwth_k}^2
  \\ \nonumber
  &
  +
  (\sigma_k-\sigma_k^o) 
  \left(
    \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    -
    \overline{\theta}_k^{2}
  \right)
  +
  \underbrace{
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
  }_{\text{see, Lemma~\ref{lem:convexity:input:constraint}}}
  \\ \nonumber
  =&
  -\underline{p}
  \norm{\mv{e}}^2
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \mm{P}
  \left(
    - \idealNN
    - \mv{\epsilon}
  \right)
  +
  \mv{e}^\top
  \Delta \mv{u}
  \\ \nonumber
  &
  -
  \sigma_k^o
  \norm{\estwth_k}^2
  -
  \sigma_k
  \overline{\theta}_k^2
  +
  \sigma_k^o
  \overline{\theta}_k^2
  +
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  \lambda_j
  c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}}
  .
\end{align}
The projection operators in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd} are omitted, according to Lemma~\ref{lem:proj:ineq}.

For the boundedness of $\idealwth_k$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$, we assume that $\NNin$ remains in the approximation region $\Omega_{\NNin}$, which will be ensured later in the proof.
Hence, $\idealwth_k$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$ are bounded by $\overline{\theta}_k^*$ and $\overline{\epsilon}$, respectively.
Similarly to the bound of $\estNN$, $\idealNN$ is bounded by $\overline{\theta}_k^* \sqrt{l_k+1}$.
Additionally, since $\mv{u}=\mm{P}\estNN=0$ when $\estwth_k=\myvec(\estwV_k)=\mv{0}$, we have $c^{u}_j(\estwth)\big\vert_{\estwth_k=\mv{0}} \le -\underline{c}_j^u < 0,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$, according to Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}. 

Using Lemma~\ref{lem:delta:u:bound}, we can further analyze $\ddtt V_{k}$ as follows:
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:2} 
  \ddtt V_{k}
  \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    -
    \sigma_k^o
    \norm{\estwth_k}^2
  \\ \nonumber
  &
    +
    \overline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}
    \left(
      \overline{\theta}^*_k
      \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
      +
      \overline{\epsilon}
      +
      \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
    \right)        
  \\ \nonumber
  &
    -
    \sigma_k 
    \overline{\theta}_k^2
    +
    \sigma_k^o 
    \overline{\theta}_k^2
    -
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    \underline{c}_j^u
    .
\end{align}

For further analysis, we apply Young's inequality which states 
% \begin{equation}
$
  ab
  \le
  \eta a^2
  +
  \frac{1}{4\eta}b^2,
  \ 
  \forall a,b\in\R, \eta\in\R_{>0}
  .
$
% \end{equation}
By applying the Young's inequality to third term in the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:Vk:dot:2}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vk:dot:3} \nonumber
  \ddtt V_{k}
  \le &
    -(\underline{p}-\eta_1-\eta_2)
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    -
    \left(
      \sigma_k^o
      \!-\!
      \tfrac{1}{4\eta_2}
      \overline{p}^2
      (l_k+1)
    \right)
    \norm{\estwth_k}^2
  \\ 
  &
    -
    \sigma_k \overline{\theta}_k^2
    -
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j
    \underline{c}_j^u
  \\ \nonumber
  &
      +
  \underbrace{
    \tfrac{1}{4\eta_1}
    \left[
    \overline{p}
      \left(
        \overline{\theta}_k^*
        \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
        +
        \overline{\epsilon}
      \right)
    \right]^2
    +
    \sigma_k^o
    \overline{\theta}_k^2
  }_{=: \delta_k \in \R_{>0}}
  ,
\end{align}
where $\eta_1,\eta_2\in\R_{>0}$ and $\sigma_k^o\in\R_{>0}$ are arbitrarily chosen such that $\underline{p}-\eta_1-\eta_2>0$ for given $\underline{p}$, and $\sigma_k^o - \frac{1}{4\eta_1} \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1) > 0$, respectively.

This result implies that $\ddtt V_{k}$ is negative outside the compact set defined by 
\begin{equation} 
  \Omega_k
  \!
  =
  \!
  \left\{
    \!
    \mv{z}_k
    \!
    :
    \!
    a
    \!
    \norm{
      \left(
        \!
        \mv{e}^\top
        \!
        ,
        \!
        \estwth_k^\top
        \!
      \right)^\top
    }^2
    \!
    +
    \!
    b
    \!
    \norm{
      \left(
        \sigma_k
        % \!
        ,
        % \!
        [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}^\top
      \right)^\top
    }_1
    \!
    \le
    \!
    \delta_k
    \!
  \right\}
  \!
  ,
\end{equation}
where
$
    a
    :=
    \min
    \left\{
        \underline{p}
        - \eta_1
        - \eta_2
        ,
        \sigma_k^o
        -
        \tfrac{1}{4\eta_1}
        \overline{p}^2
        (l_k+1)
    \right\}
    \in\R_{>0}
    ,
    b
    :=
    \min
    \left\{
        \overline{\theta}_k^2
        ,
        \min_{j\in\mathcal{I}} 
        \left\{
            \underline{c}_j^u
        \right\}
    \right\}
    \in\R_{>0}
    .
$
% and
% $
%     \mv{z}_1 
%     := 
%     \left(
%         \mv{e}^\top
%         ,
%         \estwth_k^\top
%     \right)^\top
%     ,
%     \mv{z}_2
%     :=
%     \left(
%         \sigma_k
%         ,
%         [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}^\top
%     \right)^\top
%     .
% $
Moreover, since $V_k$ is lower bounded and radially unbounded, there exists a compact sublevel set
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Sk:def}
  \mathcal S_k(\overline V_k)
  :=
  \left\{ 
    \mv{z}_k 
    : 
    V_k(\mv{z}_k)
    \le 
    \overline{V}_k 
  \right\}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{V}_k\in\R_{>0}$ is arbitrarily chosen such that $\Omega_k \subset \mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$.
Because $\ddtt V_k<0$ for all $\mv{z}_k\notin\Omega_k$, the system initialized in $\mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$ will never escape $\mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$ and eventually enter $\Omega_k$8.
Therefore, $\mv e$, $\estwth_k$, $\sigma_k$, and $\lambda_j$, $\forall j\in\mathcal I$, are uniformly ultimately bounded in $\mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$.

In addition, the boundedness of $\mv{z}_k$ implies that $\mv{x}$ is bounded, since $\mv{e}=\mv{x}-\mv{x}_d$ and $\mv{x}_d$ is bounded by Assumption~\ref{assum:xd}.
Then, by invoking the boundedness of $\mv{x}$, $\mv{x}_d$, and $\ddtt{\mv{x}}_d$, we can ensure that $\NNin$ remains in the approximation region $\Omega_{\NNin}$, which is a subset of $\mathcal{S}_k(\overline{V}_k)$.
This justifies the boundedness of $\idealwth_k$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$ used in the above analysis.

\hfill

\textit{Step~2: Boundedness of the inner layer adaptive variables.}

The remaining weights $[\estwth_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{k\}}$ and the Lagrange multipliers $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{k\}}$ are analyzed recursively from $(k-1)$th layer to the input layer ($i=0$). 
Specifically, for an arbitrary layer index $i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}$, we assume that the subsequent adaptive variables, $\estwth_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1},\cdots,\estwth_k,\sigma_k,$ have already been shown to be bounded, and then prove the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$.

Consider the Lyapunov function of $i$th layer defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Vi:def}
  V_{i}(\mv{z}_i)
  :=
  \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\estwth_i^\top\estwth_i
  +
  \tfrac{1}{\beta_i^\theta}(\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o)^2
  ,
\end{equation}
where 
$
  \mv{z}_i:=
  \left(
    \estwth_i^\top,
    \sigma_i
  \right)^\top
  ,
$
and $\sigma_i^o\in\R_{>0}$ will be determined in the subsequent analysis.
Similar to $V_k$, the weight constraint $c_i^{\theta}(\estwth_i)$ is assumed to be violated, and the projection operator in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} is omitted according to Lemma~\ref{lem:proj:ineq}.
Taking time derivative of $V_i$ yields
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vi:dot:1} 
  \ddtt V_i
  \le&
    -\sigma_i \norm{\estwth_i}^2
    -
    \estwth_i^\top
    {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
  \\ \nonumber
  &
    -
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
    \lambda_j 
    \estwth_i^\top
    {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\mv{u}}
    +
    2(\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o) c_i^{\theta}(\estwth_i)
  \\ \nonumber
  \le&
    -\sigma_i \norm{\estwth_i}^2
    +
    (\sigma_i-\sigma_i^o) 
    \left(
      \norm{\estwth_i}^2
      -
      \overline{\theta}_i^2
    \right)
  \\ \nonumber
  &
    -
    \estwth_i^\top
    \underbrace{
    \left(
      {\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}}^\top
      \mm{P}
      \left(
        \mv{e}
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j 
        \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\mv{u}}
      \right)
    \right)
    }_{=: \gamma_i}
  \\ \nonumber
  \le&
    -\sigma_i^o \norm{\estwth_i}^2
    -\sigma_i \overline{\theta}_i^2
    +
    \norm{\estwth_i}
    \norm{\gamma_i}
    +
    \sigma_i^o \overline{\theta}_i^2
  .
\end{align}

For further analysis, the boundedness of $\gamma_i$ is analyzed.
According to the DNN gradient in \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}, $\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_i}$ is bounded, since $\estwth_{i+1},\cdots,\estwth_k$ are bounded by assumption.
The remaining terms $\mv e$, $\estwth_k$, and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I}$ are bounded by the result of Step~1.
Furthermore, $\mv u$ is bounded because $\mv u$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ in \eqref{eq:NN:bound}.
In addition, since $c_j^u(\cdot)$ is smooth by Assumption~\ref{assum:sat} and $\mv u$ is bounded, $\pptfrac{c_j^u}{\mv u}$ is bounded.
Therefore, $\gamma_i$ is bounded by a unknown positive constant $\overline{\gamma}_i\in\R_{>0}$.

Then, by applying the Young's inequality to the coupled term $\norm{\estwth_i}\norm{\gamma_i}\le\norm{\estwth_i}^2+\tfrac{1}{4}\overline{\gamma}_i^2$ in \eqref{eq:Vi:dot:1}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:Vi:dot:2}
  \ddtt V_i
  \le&
    -
    \left(
      \sigma_i^o
      -
      1
    \right)
    \norm{\estwth_i}^2
    -
    \sigma_i \overline{\theta}_i^2
    +
    \delta_i
  ,
\end{align}
where $\delta_i:=\frac{1}{4}\overline{\gamma}_i^2 + \sigma_i^o \overline{\theta}_i^2$ is a positive constant, and $\sigma_i^o\in\R_{>0}$ is arbitrarily chosen such that $\sigma_i^o - 1 > 0$.
Similarly to Step~1, $\ddtt V_i$ is negative outside the compact set defined by 
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_i
  =
  \left\{
    \mv{z}_i
    :
    \left(
      \sigma_i^o
      -
      \tfrac{\overline{\gamma}_i^2}{2}
    \right)
    \norm{\estwth_i}^2
    +
    \overline{\theta}_i^2
    \norm{\sigma_i}_1
    \le
    \delta_i
  \right\}
  , 
\end{equation}
which implies the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$.
Moreover, a compact sublevel set $\mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)$ of $V_i$ can be constructed such that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Si:def}
  \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)
  :=
  \left\{
    \mv{z}_i
    :
    V_i(\mv{z}_i)
    \le
    \overline{V}_i
  \right\}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{V}_i\in\R_{>0}$ is arbitrarily chosen such that $\Omega_i \subset \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)$.
Since $\ddtt V_i<0$ for all $\mv{z}_i\notin\Omega_i$, the system initialized in $\mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)$ will remain in $\mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)$.

By recursively applying the above analysis from $i=k-1$ to $i=0$, the boundedness of $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i$ for all $i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{k\}$ can be established, which concludes the proof.

\end{proof}

% REMARKS
% ——————————————————————————————————————————————

Additionally, we introduce the following remarks regarding the initialization and the parameter tuning of CONAC.

\begin{remark}[Initialization of CONAC] \label{rem:init}
  The invariant sublevel set 
  $
    \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{K}} 
    \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)
  $ 
  used in the stability analysis depends on unknown constants.
  Therefore, an exact admissible initial domain is unavailable to compute explicitly.
  In practice, we recommend initializing; the system state $\mv{x}(0)$ close to the initial reference state $\mv{x}_d(0)$ to reduce the initial tracking error $\mv{e}(0)$; DNN weights with small random values; and zero for the Lagrange multipliers, $\sigma_i(0)=0$ and $\lambda_j(0)=0$ for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$ and $j\in\mathcal{I}$, respectively, to help ensure that the system starts within
  $
    \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{K}} 
    \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)
    .
  $
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Recommended reference trajectory design] \label{rem:ref}  
  The initialization in Remark~\ref{rem:init} helps prevent initial unstable behavior, which generally occurs by poor initial performance of NAC due to the random initialized DNN weights.
  With small random weights, the initial control input is expected to be small, thereby avoiding abrupt control actions.
  However, if the system starts near an unstable or highly nonlinear operating region, small random weights may result in unstable behavior or slow adaptation.
  Hence, it is preferable to design the reference trajectory such that the system starts in a sufficiently stable operating region, and use a conservative reference trajectory during an initial warm-up phase, as introduced in \cite{Ryu:2025aa}.
  This strategy is demonstrated across valuations in Section~\ref{sec:val}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Tuning of CONAC Parameters] \label{rem:tuning}
  The adaptation gain $\alpha$ and the multiplier update rates $\beta_i^\theta$ and $\beta_j^u$ affect the transient behavior of CONAC.
  The gain $\alpha$ determines the overall update speed of the DNN weights.
  A large $\alpha$ may improve the initial learning response but can also amplify discretization effects and induce oscillatory behavior in sampled-data implementation.
  Conversely, an excessively small $\alpha$ may lead to slow adaptation and poor transient tracking performance.

  The update rates $\beta_i^\theta$ and $\beta_j^u$ determine how rapidly the Lagrange multipliers respond to weight and input constraint violations, respectively.
  Larger values accelerate the enforcement of the corresponding constraints by increasing the multiplier response when a violation occurs.
  However, excessively large update rates may introduce abrupt changes in the adaptation law, leading to chattering or high-frequency oscillations in the control input.
  Therefore, $\alpha$, $\beta_i^\theta$, and $\beta_j^u$ should be selected to balance learning speed, prompt constraint enforcement, and smooth closed-loop behavior.
  The effect of the input constraint multiplier update rate $\beta_j^u$ is evaluated in the simulation study in Section~\ref{sec:val:results}.
\end{remark}

% -------------------------------------
% Robot System
% -------------------------------------
The two-link robotic manipulator's dynamics is described by the following Euler-Lagrange systems:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:robot:dynamics}
  \mv{M}(\mv{q})\ddot{\mv{q}}
  +
  \mv{C}(\mv{q},\dot{\mv{q}})\dot{\mv{q}}
  +
  \mv{G}(\mv{q})
  =
  \mysat
  (
  \mv{\tau}
  )
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{q} := \left(q_1,q_2\right)^\top \in \R^2$ is the joint angle vector, $\mv{\tau} := \left(\tau_1,\tau_2\right)^\top \in \R^2$ is the joint torque vector, $\mv{M}(\mv{q}) \in \R^{2\times2}$ is the inertia matrix, $\mv{C}(\mv{q},\dot{\mv{q}}) \in \R^{2\times2}$ is the Coriolis matrix, and $\mv{G}(\mv{q}) \in \R^2$ is the gravity vector.
The inertia matrix $\mv{M}(\mv{q})$ whose inverse is control effectiveness matrix, is symmetric positive definite matrix according to the physical properties of Euler-Lagrange systems, \cite[Lemma 3.1]{Ji:2025aa}.
To apply CONAC, the error dynamics of the two-link robotic manipulator is reformulated into the form of \eqref{eq:error:dyn}, \ie first-order dynamics, by defining the error state as $\mv{e} := \ddtt{\mv{q}} - \ddtt{\mv{q}}_d + \Lambda(\mv{q}-\mv{q}_d)$, where $\Lambda\in\R^{2\times2}$ is a positive definite matrix.
Then, 

the two-link robotic manipulator's dynamics is reformulated into the form of \eqref{eq:sys}, by defining the state as $\mv{x} := \ddtt\mv{q}+\Lambda\mv{q}$, and the control input as $\mv{u} := \mv{\tau}$, where $\Lambda\in\R^{2\times2}$ is a positive definite matrix.

In the input saturation $\mysat(\mv{\tau})$, the torque of the second joint $\tau_2$ is saturated in $[-\overline{\tau}_2, \overline{\tau}_2] = [\qty{-3.5}{\newton\meter}, \qty{3.5}{\newton\meter}]$, and the norm of the control input $\norm{\mv{\tau}}$ is bounded by $\overline{\tau} = \qty{11}{\newton\meter}$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp:robot:sat}.
These saturation can be physically interpreted as the torque limit of the second joint and the power source, respectively, and are denoted by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:val:cstr:input}
  c^u_{\overline{\tau}_2}(\mv{\tau}) 
  := 
  \tau_2 - \overline{\tau}_2
  ,
  \ 
  c^u_{\underline{\tau}_2}(\mv{\tau}) 
  := 
  -\tau_2 - \overline{\tau}_2
  ,
  \ 
  c^u_{\overline{\tau}}(\mv{\tau})
  := 
  \norm{\mv{\tau}}^2 - \overline{\tau}^2
  .
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the set of input constraints is defined as $\mathcal{I} = \{\overline{\tau}_2, \underline{\tau}_2, \overline{\tau}\}$.

\subsubsection*{Controller 3 \normalfont{(C$_3$)}—\textit{NAC with auxiliary system}}

As a baseline for comparison in handling input constraints, the auxiliary system used in \cite{Rezaei:2025aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa} was adopted in the proposed CONAC framework.
% Gao:2017aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa
The auxiliary system is defined by 
\begin{equation}
  \ddtt \mv{\zeta} 
  =
  -
  \mm{A}_{\zeta}
  \mv{\zeta}
  +
  \mm{B}_{\zeta}
  \Delta_{\zeta} \mv{u}
  ,
  \quad
  \zeta(0) 
  = 
  \mv{0}_{2\times 1}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{\zeta}\in\R^2$ denotes the auxiliary state which is initialized to zero, $\mm{A}_{\zeta}\in\R_{>0}^{2\times 2}$ is a stable matrix, and $\mm{B}_{\zeta}\in\R^{2\times 2}$ is a gain matrix, and $\Delta_{\zeta} u_i = u_i - \max(\underline{u}_i, \min(\overline{u}_i, u_i)),\ \forall i\in\{1,2\}$.
It is notable that $\Delta_{\zeta}\mv{u}$ is defined as the element-wise input overflow, which is different from the actual input saturation $\Delta\mv{u}$ in \eqref{eq:Vc:dot:1}, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp:sat:cmp}.

The auxiliary state was used in the adaptation law by substituting the the control error $\mv{e}$ with the auxiliary error $\mv{e}_\zeta := \mv{e} + \mv{\zeta}$ in \eqref{eq:adap:th:approx}.
The parameters of auxiliary system were chosen to balance the responsiveness to input overflow with the convergence speed of the auxiliary dynamics, as listed in Table~\ref{table:controller}.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive controller (CONAC) for unknown systems, addressing multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework. 
The stability of CONAC was analyzed using Lyapunov theory, demonstrating bounded tracking errors and the adaptive variables, including the deep neural network (DNN) weights and Lagrange multipliers, under the proposed adaptation law.
By embedding the constraints into the optimization process, CONAC ensured weight convergence in accordance with the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)-like optimality conditions, thereby ensuring both stability and optimality.

% CONAC effectively incorporated multiple convex input constraints, ensuring that actuator limitations and closed-loop stability.

Numerical simulations demonstrated the flexibility of CONAC in handling various convex input constraints simultaneously, and the effect of the update rates of the Lagrange multipliers on enforcement of input constraints.
Additionally, real-time implementation validated the feasibility of CONAC in real-world applications.

Future work may extend this approach to include constraints on both control inputs and system states, and minimize the infinite horizon error cost, thereby further enhancing the flexibility and optimality of neuro-adaptive control systems within constrained optimization frameworks.
