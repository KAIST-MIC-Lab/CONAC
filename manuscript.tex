%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
    This study presents a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) for uncertain systems subject to convex input constraints. 
    A deep neural network (DNN) is employed to approximate the ideal stabilizing control law while simultaneously learning the unknown system dynamics and addressing convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework.
    The adaptation law of DNN weights is formulated to solve the defined constrained optimization problem, ensuring satisfaction of first-order optimality conditions at steady state.
    The controller's stability is rigorously analyzed using Lyapunov theory, guaranteeing uniformly ultimately bounded tracking errors and DNN weights. 
    The proposed controller is compared with existing methods in numerical simulations, demonstrating its superiority in terms of tracking performance and input constraint satisfaction.
    The feasibility and effectiveness of CONAC in real-world applications are validated through a real-time implementation on a 2-DOF robotic manipulator.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
    Neuro-adaptive control, constrained optimization, deep neural network, convex input constraints.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{rom} a practical perspective, many engineering systems are constrained by safety requirements, actuator limitations, and physical restrictions \cite{Ji:2025aa}.
These constraints often manifest as input constraints, which can be in the form of bounds on control inputs or more complex nonlinear constraints rather than simple box constraints which limit each input bound independently.
For instance, due to power limitations for actuators, robotic manipulators may have constraints on the maximum bounds of norm of joint torques \cite{}.
Moreover, in motor drive systems, constraints on each axis's current are often forms of polyhedral sets \cite{}.
Significantly, input constraints induced by state constraints using control barrier functions (CBFs) \cite{Ames:2019aa} are inherently nonlinear and complex.
Addressing these nonlinear input constraints is crucial for ensuring the safety and reliability of control systems in practical applications.

In other aspects, uncertainties in system dynamics, such as unmodeled dynamics and state-dependent disturbances, pose significant challenges in control system design \cite{}.
Adaptive control methods have been widely studied to estimate and compensate for these uncertainties online, ensuring desired performance and stability \cite{}.
However, conventional adaptive control methods, generally, rely on predefined structures of control laws and system dynamics, which may limit their effectiveness in handling complex uncertainties.
To overcome these limitations, neuro-adaptive control (NAC) methods approximate unknown nonlinear functions using neural networks (NNs), by mapping them into a structured and parameterized representation.
NAC methods have shown promising results in various applications, including flexible robotic manipulators \cite{Gao:2026aa}, active suspensions \cite{Zhao:2016aa}, synchronous machine drives \cite{Ryu:2025aa}, and swarm control of omnidirectional mobile robots \cite{Yu:2022aa}.

Despite the successful application of NAC methods, most existing approaches primarily focus on unconstrained control problems or simple input constraints, such as box constraints \cite{}.
These methods often struggle to handle complex nonlinear input constraints, which are prevalent in practical systems, as mentioned earlier.
To address this gap, this study proposes a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) framework that integrates constrained optimization techniques with NAC methods.

\subsection{Literature Review}

\subsubsection{Input Constraints Handling}

To satisfy input constraints induced by actuator limitations and safety requirements, there are two  representative approaches in the literature: 1) optimization based methods, and 2) 
structural methods.
The optimization-based methods solve a constrained optimization problem at each time step to compute the optimal control input while satisfying the input constraints, \eg model predictive control (MPC) \cite{} and control Lyapunov function (CLF)-CBF based quadratic programming (QP) \cite{Ames:2019aa}.
Recent studies have reported the integration of NNs with MPC \cite{} and CBF \cite{} to relax conservative performance due to uncertainties.
However, these optimization-based methods typically require high computational resources and may not guarantee real-time implementation, especially for systems with fast dynamics or high-dimensional state spaces.

On the other hand, structural methods design controllers that inherently satisfy input constraints by modifying the control law structure.
Representatively, auxiliary systems are designed to reflect input saturation effects and incorporated into the control law in \cite{Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,He:2016aa,Zhao:2016aa,Gao:2026aa,Yu:2022aa}.
In general, the state of the auxiliary system is directly incorporated into control laws to attenuate the effect of input saturation.
Some studies used the auxiliary state as a feedback error to adapt the NN weights \cite{Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa}.

To explicitly handle input constraints, some studies ensures the closed-loop stability in the presence of input saturation using Lyapunov stability analysis.
In \cite{}, the control law is augmented with a smooth saturation function, \eg $\tanh(\cdot)$, to prevent input saturation.
Barrier Lyapunov functions are also employed to inherently satisfy input and state constraints \cite{}.
In other words, even if input of NAC exceeds the constraints, the closed-loop stability is still guaranteed. 

Additionally, for backstepping-based NAC methods, command filters are utilized to limit magnitude and rate of the desired virtual control inputs \cite{Chen:2011aa}.
In \cite{Gao:2006aa}, saturated amount of control input is estimated using NNs and compensated in the control law.

\MSRY{관심사를 convex saturation에 제한한다는 점을 명확히 할 것}
Both optimization-based and structural methods have shown their effectiveness and advantages in handling input constraints.
However, optimization-based methods may face challenges in real-time implementation due to computational complexity.
Structural methods, while computationally efficient, are often limited to box constraints and may not generalize well to complex nonlinear input constraints.

\subsubsection{Weight Boundedness}

Additional aspects in NAC methods include boundedness of NN weights in online adaptation.
The boundedness of NN weights should be guaranteed to ensure closed-loop stability, and prevent overfitting and parameter drift which often lead to input saturation.
Motivated by adaptive control, $\sigma$-modification \cite{He:2016aa,Gao:2006aa,Zhao:2016aa} and $\epsilon$-modification \cite{Esfandiari:2015aa,Esfandiari:2014aa} are widely used by adding stabilizing terms to the adaptation laws of NN weights.
However, these methods biases the NN weights toward origin and may degrade approximation performance \cite{Ryu:2025ab}.
Several studies simply have projected the NN weights onto predefined compact sets using projection operators \cite{}.
This approach allows the NN weights to explore without biasing, but prior knowledge of appropriate compact sets is required, which may not be feasible in practice.

\subsubsection{Potential of Constraint Optimization}

A promising approach to overcoming the limitations of the existing methods lies in constrained optimization.
By formulating the NAC problem as a unified optimization problem with constraints, it is possible to adapt the NN weights while minimizing an objective function, \eg tracking error, subject to both weight and input constraints, simultaneously.
Constrained optimization provides a theoretical framework for defining optimality and presents numerical methods for finding solutions that satisfy the constraints \cite{Nocedal:2006aa}.

This gap suggests that constrained optimization could be key to addressing both weight boundedness and input constraints in a unified, theoretically grounded framework, particularly in real-time NAC.

\section{Problem Formulation} \label{sec:problem:formulation}

We consider an unknown nonlinear system modeled as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sys}
    \ddtt\mv{x} = \mv{f}(\mv{x}) + \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u})
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{x},\mv{u}\in\R^n$ denote state and control input vectors, respectively, and $\mv{f}:\R^n\to\R^n$ and $\mm{B}:\R^n\to\R^{n\times n}$ denote unknown system function and control effectiveness matrix, respectively.
% The compact set $\Omega_x$ denotes a approximation region where NN will approximate with minimum bounded approximation error, later.
The saturation function $\mysat:\R^n\to\Omega_u$ represents input constraints, which maps input to admissible input set $\Omega_u$.

The system \eqref{eq:sys} is subject to the following assumptions.

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound}
    The control effectiveness matrix $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ is assumed to be nonsingular and bounded as   
    $
        \underline{b}\mm{I}_n
        \preceq 
        \mm{B}(\mv{x})
        \preceq 
        \bar{b}\mm{I}_n
        ,
    $
    where $\underline{b},\bar{b}\in\R_{>0}$ are known positive constants.\
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound:grad}
    There exists an unknown positive function $\beta(\mv{x}):\R^n\to\R_{>0}$ such that $\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{v}^\top\left(\ddtt\mm{B}(\mv{x})^{-1}\right)\mv{v} \le \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{v}}^2$, $\forall \mv{v}\in\R^n$ and $\forall \mv{x}\in\R^n$.
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:sat}
    The saturation function $\mysat:\R^n\to\Omega_u$ is assumed to be convex and differentiable, which is practically common in many engineering systems.
    In addition, the set $\Omega_u$ is assumed to be a closed convex set containing the origin, such that $\sup_{\mv{u}\in\Omega_u}\norm{\mv{u}} \le \overline{u}\in\R_{>0}$, for a known positive constant $\overline{u}$.
\end{assum}

\begin{remark} \label{rem:sat:comb}
    The saturation function $\mysat(\cdot)$ can be defined by multiple convex functions to represent various combinations of input constraints, \eg
    $
        \Omega_u = \bigcap_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \left\{ \mv{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n : c^{u}_j(\mv{u}) \leq 0 \right\}
        ,
    $
    where $\mathcal{I}$ is an index set, and $c^{u}_j:\R^n\to\R$ are convex functions representing each input constraint.
    This generalizes the common box constraints, allowing for more complex and realistic input constraint representations.
\end{remark}

In addition, we assume that the desired trajectory $\mv{x}_d(t):\R_{\ge0}\to\Omega_{x_d}$ is given and sufficiently smooth, and bounded, such that $\mv{x}_d\in\Omega_{x_d}:=\left\{\mv{x}_d\in\R^n : \norm{\mv{x}_d}\le \overline{x}_d\right\}$ and $\ddtt\mv{x}_d$ is bounded in a compact set $\Omega_{dx_d}$.
% It is notable that the desired trajectory $\mv{x}_d(t)$ does not necessarily belong to the set $\Omega_x$, relaxing the common assumption in the literature \cite{}, to enhance practical applicability.

\hfill

In conclusion, the control objective are
\begin{itemize}
    \item Design a NAC such that $\mv{x}(t)$ tracks $\mv{x}_d(t)$, while satisfying the convex input constraints defined by $\mysat(\cdot)$; and
    \item Ensure closed-loop stability in the presence of uncertainties and input constraints, guaranteeing that the tracking error and NN weights remain bounded.
\end{itemize}

\begin{propsit}[see {\cite[Chap. 7, Prop. 7.1.9]{Bernstein:2009aa}}] \label{propsit:kron}
	For a matrix $\mm A\in\R^{n\times m}$ and a vector $\mv b\in\mathbb R^n$, the following property holds:
	\begin{equation}
		\mm A^\top \mv b 
		= 
		\myvec(\mm A^\top\mv b)
		=
		\myvec(\mv b^\top\mm A)
		= 
		(\mm{I}_{m}\otimes \mv b^\top) \myvec(\mm A)
		.
	\end{equation}
\end{propsit}

\begin{lem}[Comparison Lemma {\cite[pp. 102--103, Lemma 3.4]{Khalil:2002aa}}] \label{lem:comp}
    Consider a scalar continuous function $V(t)\ge0,\ \forall t\in\R_{\ge0}$.
    If $V(t)$ satisfies the differential inequality    
    % \begin{equation}
    $
        \ddtt V(t) \le -a V(t) + b

    % \end{equation}
    $
    with $a,b\in\R_{>0}$, then $V(t)$ is bounded as 
    % \begin{equation}
    $
        V(t) \le \left(V(0) - \tfrac{b}{a}\right)\exp^{-at} + \tfrac{b}{a}
        ,
        \ \forall t\in\R_{\ge0}
        .

    $
\end{lem}

\end{proof}

For unknown nonlinear functions $\mv{g}:\R^{l_0+1}\to\R^{l_{k+1}}$, we employ DNNs to approximate them, \cite{Patil:2022aa}.
According to the universal approximation theorem, there exists a DNN $\NN:=\NN(\mv{x}_n;\wth):\R^{l_0+1}\times\R^{\Xi}\to\R^{l_{k+1}}$ where $\mv{x}_n\in\R^{l_0+1}$ denotes the NN input vector and $\wth\in\R^{\Xi}$ denotes the DNN weights, such that 
% \begin{equation}
$
    \sup_{\mv{x}_n\in\Omega_{x_n}}
    \norm{
        \mv{g}(\mv{x}_n) - \NN(\mv{x}_n;\wth^*)
    }
    =
    \overline{\epsilon}
    ,
$
% \end{equation}  
where $\wth^*\in\R^{\Xi}$ denotes the ideal DNN weights minimizing the approximation error $\overline{\epsilon}\in\R_{\ge0}$ and $\Omega_{x_n}\subset\R^{l_0+1}$ denotes a compact approximation region.
The DNN can be defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dnn:def}
    \NN_i :=
    \begin{cases}
        \wV_i^\top \act_i(\NN_{i-1}), 
        &
        i\in\{1,\dots ,k\}=:\mathcal{K},
        \\
        \wV_0^\top {x}_n,
        &
        i=0
        ,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\wV_i\in\R^{(l_i+1)\times l_{i+1}}$ and $\act_i: \R^{l_i}\to\R^{l_i+1}$ represent the weight matrix and the activation function of the $i$th layer, respectively, and $\mathcal{K}$ denotes the index set of layers.
The DNN $\NN({x}_n;\wth)$ has $k+1$ layers, where the input and output layers are indexed by $i=0$ and $i=k$, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:DNN} as an example.
The activation function is defined as $\act_i(\mv{z}) = \left(\tanh(z_1),\cdots,\tanh(z_{l_i}),1\right)^\top,\ \forall\mv{z}\in\R^{l_i}$.
We simplify the notations by vectorizing all weight matrices as $\wth_i := \myvec(\wV_i)\in\R^{(l_i+1)l_{i+1}},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}$, and stacking them as $\wth := \left[\wth_i\right]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$, where $\Xi := \sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}} (l_i+1)l_{i+1}$.

The gradient of $ \NN({\mv{x}}_n;\wth)$ with respect to $\wth$ can be obtained using Proposition \ref{propsit:kron} and the chain rule as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth}=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_k}&
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_{k-1}}&
    \cdots &
        \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_0}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in\R^{n \times \Xi}
    ,
    \label{eq:gradient:phi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth_i} = 
    \begin{cases}
        (
            \mm I_{l_{k+1}}
            \otimes 
            \act_{k}^\top  
        )
        , 
        &
        i=k
        ,
        \\
        \wV_k^\top\act_{k}' 
        (
            \mm I_{l_{k}}
            \otimes  
            \act_{k-1}^\top  
        )
        , 
        & 
        i=k-1
        ,
        \\
        \vdots 
        &
        \vdots 
        \\
        \wV_k^\top\act'_{k} 
        \cdots 
        \wV_1^\top\act_1' 
        (
            \mm I_{l_1}
            \otimes 
            {\mv{x}}_n^\top  
        )
        , 
        &
        i = 0
        ,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:NN:grad}
\end{equation}
and $\act_i:= \act_i(\NN_{i-1})$ and $\act_i':= \ddtfrac{\act_i}{\NN_{i-1}}$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$.

\subsection{Controller Design} \label{sec:main:result:sub:ctrl:design}

First, we define the tracking error as $\mv{e} = \mv{x} - \mv{x}_d$, and derive the error dynamics from \eqref{eq:sys} as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:error:dyn}
    \ddtt\mv{e}
    =
    \mv{f}(\mv{x}) + \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u}) - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    .
\end{equation}
Let us consider Lyapunov function $V_1:=\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}(\mv{x})\mv{e}$.
Taking time derivative of $V_1$ yields
\begin{align} \label{eq:V1:dot:1} \nonumber
    \ddtt V_1
    =&
    \tfrac{1}{2}\mv{e}^\top
    \left(
        \ddtt\mm{B}^{-1}
    \right)
    \mv{e}
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        \mv{f} + \mm{B}\mysat(\mv{u}) 
        - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    \\
    \le&
    \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mv{u}
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        \mv{f} - \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta\mv{u}
    ,
\end{align}
where arguments of $\mv{f}(\mv{x})$ and $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ are omitted for brevity and $\Delta\mv{u} := \mysat(\mv{u}) - \mv{u}$ denotes amount of input saturation.

A desired control law $\mv{u}^*$ such that the tracking error $\mv{e}$ is stable in the absence of uncertainties and input saturation, is selected as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ctrl:ideal}
    \mv{u}^*
    :=
    -
    \mm{P}
    \mv{e}
    +
    - \beta(\mv{x})\mv{e}
    +
    \mm{B}^{-1}
    \left(
        - \mv{f} 
        + \ddtt\mv{x}_d
    \right)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\mm{P}=\mm{P}^\top\in\R^{n\times n}$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix satisfying $\underline{p}\mm{I}_n \preceq \mm{P} \preceq \overline{p}\mm{I}_n$ for known positive constants $\underline{p},\overline{p}\in\R_{>0}$.

By substituting $\mv{u}^*$ \eqref{eq:ctrl:ideal} into \eqref{eq:V1:dot:1}, we obtain
\begin{equation} \label{eq:V1:dot:2}
    \ddtt V_1
    \le
    -
    \underline{p}\norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \left(
        \mv{u} - \mv{u}^*        
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta\mv{u}
    .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adaptation Laws} \label{sec:main:result:sub:adap:laws}

The control objective which minimizes the tracking error $\mv{e}$ while satisfying both weight and input constraints can be represented as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:opt:prob} 
    \begin{align} 
        \min_{\estwth} \quad 
        & 
        J(\mv{e};\estwth):= \tfrac{1}{2} \mv{e}^\top \mv{e} \label{eq:opt:prob:obj} 
        \\ 
        \text{subject to} \quad 
        & 
        c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i):=\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\estwth_i}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\theta}_i^2 \le 0, 
        \  \forall i\in\mathcal{K} \label{eq:opt:prob:const1} 
        \\ 
        & 
        c^{u}_{j}(\estwth) \le 0, 
        \quad \forall j\in\mathcal{I} \label{eq:opt:prob:const2} 
        ,
    \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $J(\mv{e};\estwth)$ is the objective function and $\mathcal{I}$ denotes the set of imposed input constraints, as described in Remark \ref{rem:sat:comb}.
Inequality constraints, \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const1} and \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2}, are imposed during the weight adaptation process.
The Lagrangian function $L:=L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})$ can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    L
    = 
    J(\mv{e};\estwth) 
    + 
    \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
    \sigma_{i}
    c^{\theta}_{i}(\estwth_i)
    +
    \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}
    \lambda_{j}
    c^{u}_{j}(\estwth)
    ,
    \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ denote the Lagrange multipliers for each weight and input constraints, respectively.

The adaptation laws for $\estwth$, and $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ are derived to solve \eqref{eq:opt:prob} by using a primal-dual gradient update law \cite{}, \ie $\ddtt\estwth = -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}$, $\ddtt\sigma_i = \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}\left(\beta_i \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i}\right)$, and $\ddtt\lambda_j = \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}\left(\beta_j \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j}\right)$, resulting in
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:adap}
    \begin{align}
            \ddtt{\estwth}
            &
            % =
            % -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}
            =
            -\alpha 
            \left(
                \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
                +
                \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
                \sigma_i 
                \estwth_i
                +
                \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
                \lambda_j 
                \pptfrac{c_j}{\estwth}
            \right),
        \label{eq:adap:th}
            \\
            \ddtt\sigma_i
            & 
            % = 
            % \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
            % \left(
            %     \beta_i \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i} 
            % \right)
            = 
            \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
                \left(
                \beta_i 
                c^{\theta}_{i} 
            \right)
            ,
            \quad \forall i\in\mathcal{K},
        \label{eq:adap:sig}
            \\
            \ddtt\lambda_j
            & 
            % = 
            % \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
            % \left(
            %     \beta_j \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j} 
            % \right)
            = 
            \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
                \left(
                \beta_j 
                c^{u}_{j} 
            \right)
            ,
            \quad \forall j\in\mathcal{I},
        \label{eq:adap:lbd}
    \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $\alpha\in\R_{>0}$ denotes the adaptation gain (learning rate) and $\beta_i,\beta_j\in\R_{>0}$ denotes the update rate of the Lagrange multipliers, and $J:=J(\mv{e})$. 
The Lagrange multipliers associated with inequality constraints are non-negative, \ie $\sigma_i,\lambda_j\ge 0$, due to the projection operator $\myproj_{(\cdot)\ge0}(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}.
\MSRY{Smooth projection!}
When a constraint becomes active, \ie non-negative constraint violation, the corresponding Lagrange multiplier increases, see \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}, to address the violation, see \eqref{eq:adap:th}.
Once the violation is resolved and the constraint is no longer active, \ie negative constraint, the Lagrange multiplier decreases gradually until it returns to zero by projection operator.

At steady state, where $\ddtt{\estwth}=0$, $\ddtt\sigma_i=0$ and $\ddtt\lambda_j=0$, the KKT conditions are satisfied, \ie $\pptfrac{L}{\estwth}=0$, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j \le 0$, $\sigma_i,\lambda_j \ge 0$, and $\sigma_i c^{\theta}_i=0$, and $\lambda_j c^{u}_j=0$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$ and $j\in\mathcal{I}$.
In other words, the proposed adaptation laws update $\estwth$, and $\sigma_i$ and $\lambda_j$ in a way that satisfies the KKT conditions at steady state.
These conditions represent the first-order necessary conditions for optimality, guiding the updates toward candidates for a locally optimal point.

\hfill

The adaptation law of $\estwth$ defined in \eqref{eq:adap:th} involves the partial derivative of the error with respect to control input $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\rbu}$, \ie 
$
    \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
    =
    \left(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        (\pptfrac{\mv{u}}{\estwth})
    \right)
    ^\top \mv{e}
    =
    \left(
        (\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}})
        \mm{P}
        (\pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth})
    \right)
    ^\top \mv{e}
$. 
Since the objective function depends on $\mv{e}$ of a dynamic system, obtaining $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ is not straightforward. 
Besides exact methods to compute $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ such as the forward sensitivity method \cite{}, several approximation methods have been proposed in the literature \cite{}, since exact methods are often computationally expensive and impractical for real-time applications with numerous DNN weights.
In the literature, the authors approximate $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$ by taking the sign of each entry, \ie
$
    \pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}
    \approx
    \left[
        \mysign
        \left(
            \pptfrac{e_i}{u_j}
        \right)
    \right]_{i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}}
$.
However, this approach is not applicable to \eqref{eq:sys}.
Instead, since the sign of the control input matrix is known—owing to Assumption \ref{assum:B:bound}—we approximate $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}\approx \mm{I}_n$.
Consequently, the adaptation law in \eqref{eq:adap:th} becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \ddtt{\estwth}
    \approx
    -\alpha 
    \left(
        {\pptfrac{\estNN}
        {\estwth}}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \mv{e}
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
        \sigma_i 
        \estwth_i
        +
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j 
        \pptfrac{c_j}{\estwth}
    \right)
    .
    \label{eq:adap:th:approx}
\end{equation}

    Furthermore, the approximated adaptation law \eqref{eq:adap:th:approx} coincides with the various existing adaptation laws in the literature when there are no constraints, \ie $\sigma_i=0,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}$ and $\lambda_j=0,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$, derived using Lyapunov stability theory \cite{} and static approximation methods \cite{}.

\section{Stability Analysis} \label{sec:stability:analysis}

In this section, we analyze the stability of the closed-loop system under the proposed CONAC.

\hfill

To facilitate the stability analysis, we only consider the tracking error and the output layer weights, $\estwth_k$, since the activation functions are bounded, such that $\norm{\act_i}\le \sqrt{l_{k}+1}$, owing to the use of $\tanh(\cdot)$ in $\act_i(\cdot)$, which allows $\norm{\estNN}$ to be bounded by $\norm{\estwth_k}$.
Additionally, since $\estNN = \pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_k}\estwth_k = (\mm{I}_{l_{k+1}} \otimes \act_k^\top) \estwth_k$ (\ie see \eqref{eq:gradient:phi}), $\estNN$ is linearly dependent on $\estwth_k$, implying the convexity of $c^{\theta}_k(\estwth_k)$ and $c^{u}_j(\estwth),\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$, hold with respect to $\estwth_k$.

We first consider the following Lyapunov function $V_2(\mv{z}):=V_1+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\estwth_k^\top\estwth_k$, where $\mv{z} := \left(\mv{e}^\top , \estwth_k^\top\right)^\top\in\R^{n+\Xi_k}$.
Without loss of generality, we assume that weight constraint $c_k^{\theta}$ is active, \ie $\sigma_k>0$.
Taking time derivative of $V_2$, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:V2:dot:1} 
    \ddtt V_2
    \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top\mm{P}
    \left(
        \estNN - \idealNN
        - \mv{\epsilon}
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta \mv{u}
    \\ \nonumber
    &
    -
    \underbrace{
        \estwth_k^\top
        \pptfrac{\estNN}{\estwth_k}^\top
        \mm{P}
        \mv{e}
    }_{
        = \estNN^\top\mm{P}\mv{e}
    }
    -
    \sigma_k
    \estwth_k^\top
    \estwth_k
    -
    \underbrace{
        \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
        \lambda_j
        \estwth_k^\top
        \pptfrac{c^{u}_j}{\estwth_k}
    }_{\ge 0, \text{convexity of $c^{u}_j$ w.r.t. $\estwth_k$}}
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \mm{P}
    \left(
        - \idealNN
        - \mv{\epsilon}
    \right)
    +
    \mv{e}^\top
    \Delta \mv{u}
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
    -
    \underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    -
    \sigma_k \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    +
    \norm{\mv{e}}
    \overline{p}
    \left(
        \overline{\theta}_k
        \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
        +
        \overline{\epsilon}
        +
        \norm{\Delta \mv{u}}
    \right)
    .
\end{align}

This result concludes that if input saturation is not occurred, \ie $\Delta \mv{u} = \mv{0}$, the tracking error $\mv{e}$ converges to a compact set 
$
    \Omega^{e}_1 
    := 
    \left\{ 
        \mv{e} 
        : 
        \norm{\mv{e}} \le \tfrac{\overline{p}}{\underline{p}}
        \left(
            \overline{\theta}_k
            \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
            +
            \overline{\epsilon}
        \right)
    \right\}
    ,
$
while $\Omega^{e}_1$ is proportionally enlarged by $\norm{\Delta \mv{u}}$ when input saturation occurs, resulting in the compact set
$
    \Omega^{e}_2 
    := 
    \left\{ 
        \mv{e} 
        : 
        \norm{\mv{e}} \le \tfrac{\overline{p}}{\underline{p}}
        \left(
            \overline{\theta}_k
            \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
            +
            \overline{\epsilon}
            +
            \norm{\Delta \mv{u}}
        \right)
    \right\}
    .
$
The input saturation $\norm{\Delta \mv{u}}$ is upper bounded by $\norm{\theta_k}$, since $\norm{\Delta\mv{u}} \le \norm{\estNN - \overline{u}} \le \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k} - \overline{u}$ when input saturation occurs, whereas in the absence of input saturation, $\norm{\Delta \mv{u}} = 0$.
Hence, the ultimate bound of $\estwth_k$ into a compact set with radius $\overline{\theta}_k$ is guaranteed by $\sigma_k>0$ due to the weight constraint $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)= \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\estwth_k}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\overline{\theta}_k^2 \le 0$.

When input saturation occurs, by substituting $\norm{\Delta \mv{u}}\le\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k} - \overline{u}$ into \eqref{eq:V2:dot:1}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:V2:dot:2}
    \ddtt V_2
    \le&
    -\underline{p}
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    -\sigma_k \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    \\ \nonumber
    &
    +
    \norm{\mv{e}}
    \overline{p}
    \left(
        \overline{\theta}_k
        \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
        +
        \overline{\epsilon}
        +
        \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
        -
        \overline{u}
    \right)
    \\ \nonumber
    \le&
    -(\underline{p}-1)
    \norm{\mv{e}}^2
    +
    -(\sigma_k - \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1))
    \norm{\estwth_k}^2
    \\
    &
    +
    \overline{p}
    \left(
        \overline{\theta}_k
        \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
        +
        \overline{\epsilon}
        +
        \overline{u}
    \right)^2
    ,
\end{align}
which implies that $\mv{e}$ and $\estwth_k$ are UUB if $\underline{p} > 1$ and $\sigma_k > \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$.
Therefore, $\sigma_k$ should increase larger than $\overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$ to guarantee the UUB of $\mv{e}$ and $\estwth_k$ when input saturation occurs.
It is worth to note that, while $\sigma_k$ increases, the radius of $\Omega_2^{e}$ increases temporarily by $\Delta\mv{u}$, since boundedness of $\estwth_k$ is not guaranteed until $\sigma_k$ exceeds $\overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$.

\hfill

The finite time of reaching condition, $\sigma_k > \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$, and finite expansion of the radius of $\Omega_2^{e}$ can be guaranteed, using contradiction.
Assume that $\sigma_k < \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$ holds for all time.
Then, from \eqref{eq:adap:sig}, $\sigma_k$ is positive only if average constraint violation in time interval $[t_0, t_0+T]$, for any $t_0\ge0$ and $T>0$, is positive, \ie $\tfrac{1}{T} \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} \beta^{\theta}_k c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k) \der t \ge \beta^{\theta}_k \underline{c}_k > 0$, which leads to $\tfrac{\sigma_k(t_0+T)}{\beta^{\theta}_k \underline{c}_k} \ge T$, with $\sigma_k(t_0)=0$.
This implies when $\sigma_k$ reaches $\overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$, the time taken is bounded by $\tfrac{\overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)}{\beta^{\theta}_k \underline{c}_k} \ge \tfrac{\sigma_k(t_0+T)}{\beta^{\theta}_k \underline{c}_k} \ge T$, which contradicts the assumption.
Additionally, since $\ddtt \estwth_k$ is bounded by $\norm{\estwth_k}$ (note that $\mv{e}$ is bounded in $\Omega_2^{e}$ by $\norm{\estwth_k}$) from \eqref{eq:adap:th:approx}, $\norm{\estwth_k}$ is bounded in finite time $T$, which leads to finite expansion of the radius of $\Omega_2^{e}$.
On the other hand, if the average constraint violation is negative, then $\sigma_k$ is projected to zero, which implies that weight $\estwth_k$ is bounded by $c_k^{\theta}(\estwth_k)$.

\hfill

If $\sigma_k > \overline{p}^2 (l_k+1)$, the ultimate bound of $\mv{e}$ is obtained as 
$
    \Omega_3
    :=
    \left\{
        \mv{e}
        :
        \norm{\mv{e}}
        \le
        \tfrac{\overline{p}}{\underline{p}-1}
        \left(
            \overline{\theta}_k
            \sqrt{l_{k}+1}
            +
            \overline{\epsilon}
            +
            \overline{u}
        \right)
    \right\}
$,

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive controller (CONAC) for unknown Euler-Lagrange systems, addressing both weight norm and convex input constraints within a unified optimization framework. 
The stability of CONAC was analyzed using Lyapunov theory, demonstrating bounded tracking errors and weight estimation under real-time adaptation.

CONAC effectively incorporated both convex input constraints and weight norm constraints, ensuring that actuator limitations and neural network weights remained within predefined bounds. 
By embedding these constraints into the optimization process, CONAC ensured weight convergence in accordance with the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, thereby ensuring both stability and optimality.

Real-time implementation validated the superior performance of CONAC compared to a conventional method. 
CONAC successfully handled complex input constraints while rigorously managing weight norms, resulting in improved tracking accuracy and stable behavior without significant oscillations.

Future work may extend this approach to include constraints on both control inputs and system states, further enhancing the flexibility and robustness of neuro-adaptive control systems within constrained optimization frameworks.
